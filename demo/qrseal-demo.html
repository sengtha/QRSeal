A verified signature is not a reason to pay.

This page will never show you a green tick, because the library it runs never returns one. Verification answers a narrow question — was this code produced by a registered issuer and unaltered since — and says nothing about whether the person who showed it to you is entitled to your money. The most common QR fraud uses a perfectly genuine code.

What the parser sees

EMVCo payloads are tag, length, value, repeated. A strict parser reads two length digits and walks. Currency sits at tag 53, the amount at 54, and the signature in templates 85–87.

Three characters, four thousand times the money

Both of these are genuine. Same payee, same account, same number, both signed by the same registered issuer, both verify. Cambodia circulates riel and US dollars side by side at roughly 4,000 to 1, and tag 53 is the only thing that differs.

This is why the library resolves 116 to KHR and returns null rather than guessing an unknown code, and why the specification makes showing the amount and an alphabetic currency a MUST before authorisation. A payer cannot read 116. It is the one attack in which a valid signature actively helps the attacker.

The other three are roundtrip cases, which need the issuer's private key, and a verifier has no business loading one. Most cases are negative: what a verifier must refuse is the harder half of the specification, and the easier half to get wrong.

What this does not reach

QRSeal closes forgery at the code layer: without a registered key you cannot produce a payment code that verifies, and you cannot alter a genuine one by a single character. Everything above demonstrates that, and the demonstration is worth exactly what it claims and no more.

It does not touch authorised push payment fraud — a real, correctly registered, correctly signed code presented with a lie (“scan to receive your refund”, “scan to release your parcel”). The victim authorises the payment themselves. Nothing in the payload distinguishes that from an ordinary purchase, and no amount of cryptography can, because the deception happens one layer above the signature. That is the project's argument, not a caveat to it: the specification is one half, and the institutional measures — screening at the moment of payment, a right to contest a listing, exit controls on cash-out — are the other.

This is a demonstration. The trust list here is signed by the repository's published test keys, whose private halves are public and protect nothing. Nothing on this page is issued by, endorsed by, or agreed with the National Bank of Cambodia or any other authority. KH-SQR is proposed, not adopted.
