\title{\textbf{Signing the Instrument, Not the Transaction}\\[0.4em]
  \large Layered trust failure in national QR payment systems,\\
  with a reference implementation for Cambodia}

\author{Chay Sengtha\thanks{National University of Management, Phnom Penh.
Correspondence: \texttt{sengtha@gmail.com}.}}

\date{August 2026\\[0.5em]\normalsize\emph{Preprint. Comments welcome.}}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Cambodia's KHQR, the national EMVCo-based payment QR operated on the National
Bank of Cambodia's Bakong system, has been adopted widely enough that the public
now treats \emph{any} QR code as a payment instrument and extends to it the
trust it holds in the central bank. Attackers exploit this in two ways that are
routinely conflated: forgery, where a code is not what it claims to be, and
deception, where a genuine code, correctly signed and payable to a correctly
registered account, is presented under a false pretext.

We specify and implement KH-SQR, a signature scheme for both payment codes and
document credentials, and we use it as evidence for a claim about where its own
assurance stops. Cryptography closes forgery completely and does not touch
deception at all, because a control enforced at one layer does not govern
meaning created at the layer above it. Signing the code secures the instrument;
it cannot secure the transaction, because the deception occurs one layer up, in
the person. This is the same shape as the central finding of our earlier work on
sovereign hybrid blockchains, where base-layer identity secured custody of an
asset but not exposure to it, because the derivative claim was minted above the
enforcement point.

The contribution is fourfold: a four-layer decomposition of QR payment trust
that locates precisely where cryptographic assurance ends; a working,
MIT-licensed reference implementation with a language-neutral conformance suite
of forty-four cases, thirty-three of them negative; an explicit account of what the
design does not achieve and why each residual risk is unreachable from the code
layer; and a constructive corollary --- that where a control cannot be enforced
below the harm, four moves remain, acting at successive points of the causal
chain --- raise the control above the meaning, reverse the transfer, remove the
convertibility of the proceeds, or reallocate the loss. We give the first concretely as
a categorical national prohibition on URL-bearing payment and official QR codes,
paired with a public rule that requires no judgement to apply, and we use Japan, where the same QR code sits on a
reversible stored-value substrate whose illiquidity is statutory rather than
designed, to identify the properties of the payment substrate --- not the
strength of any cryptography --- as what governs exposure. We report that the gap between institutional control and public
credulity widens structurally, and that this design widens the part of it the
design cannot close: a code that visibly verifies manufactures the confidence
the unaddressed attack depends on. We report where our own work fell short of its
specification: the reference deployment does not meet the mirror-independence
requirement, and the first container encoding was not readable by a conforming
EMVCo parser --- a defect we have since corrected in a second encoding, at a cost
of sixty-four characters and one QR symbol version. And we treat the absence of
Cambodia-specific incident data as a finding rather than a gap to be filled with
vendor figures.

The institutional proposals are offered as reference designs, not as evaluated
interventions. None has been trialled, their costs are unquantified, and we say
throughout which claims rest on structure and which would need measurement this
paper does not have. What we do claim is narrower and, we think, sufficient: the
problem is present now, its trajectory is upward for reasons that do not
reverse, and asking the public to be more careful cannot reach it.
\end{abstract}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} QR payments, authorised push payment fraud, digital
signatures, trust boundaries, EMVCo, Cambodia, layered security.}

\section{Introduction}

A QR code is a picture of some bytes. Nothing about the picture indicates who
produced it, and nothing about the act of scanning it indicates why. Both facts
are obvious when stated and neither is available to a person holding a phone in
front of a printed square in a market.

Cambodia is an unusually clear case of what follows. KHQR, the national
merchant-presented payment standard operated on the National Bank of Cambodia's
Bakong system, has succeeded on a scale that is easy to understate. The
NBC reports approximately 1{,}325.65 million Bakong transactions in 2025, a
factor of 1.2 over the previous year, and around 18.9 million e-wallet accounts
\citep{nbc2025annual} --- in a country of roughly seventeen million people.
Payment by QR is not a niche behaviour in Cambodia; for a large part of the
population it is simply what paying is.

That success has a second-order effect which is the subject of this paper. When
a single visual form becomes synonymous with a single action, the form stops
carrying information. ``QR'' and ``payment'' have collapsed into one another in
public understanding, and with them ``QR'' and ``sanctioned by the central
bank''. The result is a divergence between two boundaries that a designer would
like to coincide:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item the \textbf{trust boundary}: the set of artefacts whose provenance the
    NBC and its participants actually control and can vouch for;
  \item the \textbf{credulity boundary}: the set of artefacts to which the
    public extends the trust it holds in the NBC.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The second is much larger than the first, and it is the difference
between them that attackers operate in. It is also widening: the two boundaries
grow at different rates and only one of them can contract, which
Section~\ref{sec:trajectory} argues is the more consequential fact about this
system's future than anything in its present. A sticker over a merchant's code is
inside the gap. So is a printed code on a leaflet claiming to verify a document.
So, crucially, is a completely genuine KHQR presented with a false story
attached.

Signing the codes closes part of this gap and, we will argue, exactly the part
that is easiest to close and least often exploited. The rest is not a
cryptographic problem, and the central claim of this paper is that it cannot be
made into one:

\begin{quote}
\emph{A control enforced at one layer does not govern meaning created at the
layer above it.}
\end{quote}

\noindent Instantiated here: a signature proves who issued a code and cannot
address why the payer scanned it. We regard this as a general result about
layered systems rather than a local observation about Cambodia, and
Section~\ref{sec:general} argues the generalisation, including its relationship
to the custody/exposure finding in our earlier work on regulated DeFi
\citep{sengtha2026csb} and its applicability to other real-time push-payment
markets.

We are deliberate about what this paper is not. It is not a claim to have solved
QR phishing. A reviewer who finishes it believing that has been given the wrong
paper, and we have written Section~\ref{sec:notachieved} --- the longest
substantive section --- to make that reading difficult.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item A four-layer decomposition of QR payment trust (Section~\ref{sec:layers})
    in which each layer's residual risk motivates the next, and in which the
    boundary between layers two and three is identified as the point where
    cryptography stops.
  \item KH-SQR: a specification and a working, MIT-licensed reference
    implementation covering a payment profile bound to EMVCo payloads and a
    standalone credential profile, with a language-neutral conformance suite of
    forty-four cases, thirty-three negative, across both container encodings
    \citep{khsqr2026repo}.
  \item An explicit account of the residual risk (Section~\ref{sec:notachieved}),
    explaining for each item \emph{why} it is unreachable from the code layer
    rather than merely noting that it is, and an institutional-layer design
    (Section~\ref{sec:institutional}) that addresses part of it by
    non-cryptographic means.
  \item A policy instrument (Section~\ref{sec:categorical}) that reaches an
    attack the software cannot: a categorical prohibition on URL-bearing QR
    codes across the regulated perimeter, paired with an unconditional public
    rule. We show that every software alternative fails at the same point ---
    it requires a judgement before or during the scan --- and that the rule must
    not be scoped, because scoping it would require the user to classify a code
    before it is readable.
  \item Two honest negative results about our own work. The reference deployment
    does not satisfy the mirror-independence requirement the specification itself
    states. And our first container encoding was not transparent to a strict
    EMVCo parser, contrary to the design's premise for choosing the unreserved
    template range --- a defect we repaired rather than annotated, by specifying
    and implementing a second encoding (Section~\ref{sec:container}) that stays
    within EMVCo at a cost of 64 characters and one QR symbol version. Encoding
    version~1 is frozen for new issuance and still verified, so a conforming
    verifier must support both and dispatch on the encoding rather than attempt
    either with the other's rules.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{On evidence.}
There is no reliable Cambodia-specific data on QR-code fraud. We searched for
it and did not find it, and we regard that absence as a finding worth stating
(Section~\ref{sec:data}) rather than a gap to be papered over with the vendor
figures currently in circulation. Where we make a technical claim, it is
supported either by a citation to a primary specification or by an artefact in
the repository that a reader can execute.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{KHQR and Bakong}

KHQR follows the EMVCo merchant-presented QR specification
\citep{emvco2017mpm}, which defines a payload as a flat sequence of
ID/Length/Value data objects. Both the ID and the Length are two decimal
digits; a Length ranges from ``01'' to ``99'', so no single data object may
carry more than 99 characters of value. The payload ends with a CRC-16 in tag
\code{63}. IDs \code{80} to \code{99} are ``unreserved templates'', available
for payment systems and value-added service providers to place their own data,
each required to carry a Globally Unique Identifier at sub-tag \code{00} that
establishes the template's namespace. These details matter to
Section~\ref{sec:design} and, uncomfortably for us, to
Section~\ref{sec:container}.

Bakong is the settlement layer beneath it. Its scale is documented in the NBC's
annual reporting \citep{nbc2025annual}; the property that matters for this
paper is not scale but \emph{finality}. A KHQR payment is a push payment: the
payer initiates and authorises a transfer to a payee, and once made it is
final. There is no chargeback rail comparable to card networks'.

\subsection{Irrevocability and the shape of the fraud economy}

Irrevocability is a design virtue with consequences on both sides of the ledger.
For merchants it removes chargeback risk and settlement delay, which is a
substantial part of why acceptance costs are low enough for a market stall. For
fraud it removes the recovery mechanism that card networks use to make deception
partially reversible.

The consequence is structural: in a push-payment system, an attacker who can
persuade a victim to authorise a payment has already won, and no property of the
payment instrument can undo it. Effort therefore migrates away from compromising
the instrument and towards compromising the persuasion. This is not a
prediction; it is the observed trajectory in every real-time push-payment market
with published statistics, and it is why the United Kingdom eventually addressed
its equivalent problem through liability allocation rather than instrument
security \citep{psr2024app}.

\subsection{The QR trust model, such as it is}

The trust model a user actually operates with, when scanning a code, is roughly:
\emph{this square was placed here by someone entitled to place it, and doing
what it says is what I intended to do}. Neither clause is verifiable by
inspection. A QR code is not human-readable; there is no equivalent of noticing
that a URL is misspelled, because there is no visible URL.

Existing mitigations largely attempt to move the decision into a channel the
user can inspect --- displaying the destination domain before opening it, showing
the payee name before confirming. These help, and Section~\ref{sec:notachieved}
gives the empirical reason to expect them to help less than designers hope.

\section{Threat decomposition}
\label{sec:layers}

We decompose the trust question into four layers. The decomposition is not
merely descriptive: each layer's residual risk is what motivates the next, and
the boundary between the second and third is the subject of the paper.

\begin{widetable}
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.13\linewidth}p{0.24\linewidth}p{0.26\linewidth}p{0.28\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Layer} & \textbf{Question} & \textbf{Mechanism} & \textbf{Residual risk} \\
\midrule
1. Code authenticity &
  Was this code produced by a registered issuer, unaltered? &
  Digital signature over the payload; trust list; revocation &
  The verifier itself may be counterfeit \\[0.4em]
2. Verifier authenticity &
  Is the application making the claim genuine? &
  Application trust list; platform distribution; attestation &
  A counterfeit verifier the user installed and trusts reports whatever its
  author chose \\[0.4em]
3. Payee legitimacy &
  \emph{Should} this money go to this account? &
  Registration controls; account risk lists; behavioural analytics &
  Legitimacy is a fact about intent and history, not about bytes \\[0.4em]
4. Recovery &
  What happens after a loss? &
  Liability allocation; reimbursement; freeze and recall procedures &
  Recovery is partial, slow, and after the fact \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Four layers of QR payment trust. Cryptography is decisive at layers~1
and~2 and silent at layers~3 and~4.}
\label{tab:layers}
\end{widetable}

\paragraph{Layer 1 --- code authenticity.}
Solvable, and solved by the design in Section~\ref{sec:design}. A signature over
the payload, verified against a trust list rooted in an offline key, establishes
that a registered issuer produced exactly these bytes. Within the standard
cryptographic threat model this is complete: an attacker cannot forge a
signature, and any modification invalidates one.

That model should be stated where the claim is made rather than corrected later.
``Complete'' means complete \emph{inside a conforming verifier running on an
uncompromised device}. A counterfeit verifier (layer~2), a code the handset's
camera opens without reaching any verifier at all
(Section~\ref{sec:scanpath}), and a device the operator controls
(Section~\ref{sec:device}) each void the guarantee without touching the
mathematics. The forgery result is unconditional about bytes and conditional on
everything that reads them.

\paragraph{The layer is empty in practice, and not only in Cambodia.} It is
worth establishing that this is a gap rather than a solved problem others have
addressed. Japan's unified merchant-presented guideline, which governs a
national scheme of comparable ambition and derives from the same EMVCo
specification, defines \emph{no} signature, message authentication code or
cryptographic integrity mechanism over the payload: integrity is the CRC at
ID~\code{63} and nothing further \citep{jpqrmpm}. A CRC detects accidental
corruption and is trivially recomputed by anyone who alters a payload
deliberately, which is what makes it a checksum rather than a control. Two
national schemes, two guidelines, the same absence.

\paragraph{Layer 2 --- verifier authenticity.}
Partially solvable. A signature is only as useful as the software reporting it,
and a counterfeit wallet that draws a green tick for every code defeats layer~1
entirely without breaking any cryptography. Mitigations exist --- an application
trust list, platform distribution channels, device attestation --- and all of them
are weaker than layer~1's, because they depend on the user having obtained the
verifier through a trustworthy path.

\paragraph{The boundary.}
Between layers~2 and~3 the question changes kind. Layers~1 and~2 ask whether an
artefact is what it claims to be: a question about bytes, with a decidable
answer, computable offline from the artefact and a public key. Layer~3 asks
whether a payment \emph{ought} to be made: a question about intent, history and
context, whose answer is not a function of the artefact at all.

No amount of additional signing crosses this boundary, because there is nothing
on the far side to sign. A genuine merchant's code, correctly signed, remains
correctly signed when a criminal photographs it and pastes it onto a fake
utility-bill notice. Every byte is authentic. The fraud is entirely in the
sentence printed above the square.

\paragraph{Layer 3 --- payee legitimacy.}
Not solvable at the code layer, addressable in part at the institutional layer
by mechanisms that are emphatically not cryptography: registration controls,
account risk lists, behavioural detection. Section~\ref{sec:institutional}
specifies our contribution here.

\paragraph{Layer 4 --- recovery.}
Not a security control at all but an allocation of loss. Its design determines
who bears the cost of the residue the first three layers leave, and therefore
who has the incentive to reduce it.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

KH-SQR defines two profiles. The full normative text is in the repository's
\code{SPEC.md} \citep{khsqr2026repo}; we describe here the decisions that carry
argumentative weight.

\subsection{Profile A: payment}
\label{sec:profileA}

Profile A adds a signature to an existing EMVCo merchant-presented payload using
template \code{85} from the unreserved range, carrying a format version, a key
identifier, an algorithm identifier, an issuance time, an optional expiry, a
payee class, and a 128-character signature at sub-tag \code{99}.

\paragraph{The signing input is a prefix.}
The signed region is defined as the ASCII octets of the payload from position
zero up to \emph{and including} the five characters \code{99128} --- sub-tag
\code{99}'s tag and its length declaration --- excluding the signature itself and
the trailing CRC. Two ordering rules make this well defined: template \code{85}
must be the last data object before the CRC, and sub-tag \code{99} must be last
within it.

The consequence is that a verifier recovers the signed region with a substring
operation and never by re-serialising parsed fields. There is no canonical form
for two implementations to disagree about, and therefore no canonicalisation
step in which to have a bug. This is the single most important structural
decision in the design, and it is a deliberate rejection of the pattern --- parse,
normalise, re-serialise, verify --- that has produced a long history of signature
bypasses in XML, JSON and JWT processing.

The suite pins the two ways an attacker might exploit a prefix rule.
\code{A-reject-\allowbreak mutation-\allowbreak outside-\allowbreak signed-\allowbreak region} appends or alters data after the
signature and must still be rejected, because the CRC covers the whole payload
even though the signature does not; \code{A-reject-\allowbreak template-\allowbreak not-last} and
\code{A-reject-\allowbreak subtag-\allowbreak not-last} reject a payload in which the signature template
is not final, which is what would otherwise let an attacker extend the payload
while keeping the signed prefix intact.

\paragraph{Raw signatures, not DER.}
Signatures are the P1363 concatenation $r \Vert s$, 64 bytes, rendered as 128
uppercase hexadecimal characters. DER is prohibited. DER's length is variable,
which would defeat the fixed-offset property above, and DER length parsing is
itself a recurring source of defects. The choice also happens to eliminate a
conversion step entirely on the Web Crypto platform, whose ECDSA operations
consume and produce exactly this representation \citep{webcrypto,fips1865}.

\paragraph{Uppercase hexadecimal, not base64.}
QR alphanumeric mode encodes two characters in 11 bits and admits only digits,
uppercase letters, space and eight further punctuation marks
\citep{iso18004}. A single lowercase character forces a byte-mode segment at 8
bits per character. Base64's mixed case would therefore enlarge every signed
symbol.

\paragraph{Static and dynamic codes.}
A dynamic code carries an expiry no more than 300 seconds after issuance. A
static code carries no amount, so that a printed sticker cannot be given a
figure the merchant did not choose.

\subsection{Profile B: credential}

Profile B signs a document attestation as a standalone payload:
\begin{gather*}
\text{claims (CBOR)} \rightarrow \text{COSE\_Sign1 (ES256)} \rightarrow{} \\
\text{deflate (zlib)} \rightarrow \text{base45} \rightarrow
\text{prefix \code{KH1:}}
\end{gather*}

The pipeline is the EU Digital COVID Certificate's, with a different prefix and
claim set \citep{eudcc2021,rfc9052,rfc9053,rfc8949,rfc1950,rfc1951,rfc9285}. We
say this plainly because the originality of this work is not in the primitives
or the pipeline; it is in the composition and in the analysis of what the
composition cannot do. Base45 rather than base64 for the same alphanumeric-mode
reason as above --- the choice the DCC made, for the same reason.

\paragraph{Excluding URL carriers.}
A Profile B payload must not be an \code{http} or \code{https} URL, and a
verifier must reject one. This is a design constraint, not an omission. A
URL-bearing QR relocates the trust decision into a browser and asks the user to
judge a domain name, which is the judgement that Section~\ref{sec:notachieved}
gives empirical reason to believe people do not reliably make. Our
implementation exposes this check as a function intended to be applied to
\emph{every} scanned code, not only to KH-SQR ones.

\paragraph{The transplant attack, enforced in the API shape.}
\label{sec:transplant}
A valid signature proves a credential was issued. It does not prove the
credential belongs to the paper it is printed on. A genuine QR code photographed
from a real degree certificate and printed onto a forged one verifies perfectly,
because nothing about the paper is signed.

The only defence is comparison of the signed fields against the visible
document, and a defence that depends on a caller remembering to perform it will
not be performed. The specification therefore constrains the API rather than
merely advising: verification must return a structure carrying the subject name,
document identifier, issuing organisation and issue date in a form the caller
must handle; it must not return a boolean; and it must not expose an
\code{isValid} accessor by which a caller could reach a verdict without them. In
the reference implementation the field is named \code{mustMatchPrintedDocument},
and a test asserts that no boolean member exists on the result type --- so that a
later refactor adding a convenience accessor fails the build rather than
silently removing the only defence.

\subsection{Carrying the credential, or carrying a reference to it}
\label{sec:verifygov}

Profile B is not proposed into a vacuum. Cambodia operates \code{verify.gov.kh},
a national platform that places QR codes on diplomas, medical licences,
certificates of incorporation, land titles and civil status documents; it was
built by a Cambodian technical team, received the 2026 United Nations Public
Service Award, and has been adopted by Timor-Leste
\citep{cambodianess2026verifygov,khmertimes2026verifygov}. We do not audit it
here --- little technical documentation is public, and a comparison built on
inference would not be worth reading. One architectural fact is enough, and it
is settled from an artefact rather than from prose: the code on an issued degree
carries a URL of the shape \code{https://\allowbreak verify.gov.kh/\allowbreak verify/}$\langle$64
hex$\rangle$\code{?key=}$\langle$64 hex$\rangle$, with no signature and nothing a
verifier could check offline.\footnote{We did not resolve the URL, which
addresses a named individual's record, and we record its structure only.}

That is the distinction this section is about, and it generalises past any one
platform. A scanned code can carry the credential or a reference to it. The
choice is architectural: rendering, field selection and interface copy are all
revisable between releases, but which of those two things is in the symbol is
not.

\paragraph{What a reference costs.} It binds verification to a live service, so
the credential's lifetime becomes the service's. A degree must verify in forty
years and a land title in a hundred, and over those spans the guarantee stops
being cryptographic and becomes organisational --- that some institution keeps
the same service reachable at the same name. The obligation is already large:
more than 418{,}000 grade~12 certificates were issued this way between 2022 and
2025, and one university reports 12{,}351 degrees between 2023 and 2026
\citep{khmertimes2026verifygov}. Crossing a border sharpens the dependency
rather than easing it, and the platform is being shared internationally under a
stated aim that a document issued in one country be verifiable in another
\citep{khmertimes2026verifygov}: a Cambodian degree shown to an employer abroad
in 2050 then verifies only while a foreign ministry's servers remain reachable,
unblocked and answering --- a dependency the holder cannot control and the
relying party cannot audit. A signature inverts it. The relying party needs the
issuer's archived public key and nothing else, which is why the EU chose one for
a credential checked across borders by parties with no relationship to the
issuer \citep{eudcc2021}.

A reference with a capability in it costs something further. Where the URL
carries a secret, possession of the link is possession of the record: a
photograph of the certificate --- sent to an employer, posted to a profile,
forwarded through a messaging application --- carries a working key, and the copy
is functionally the original. Path and query components are not confidential the
way a request body is; they appear in browser history, server and proxy logs and
link previews. The leak is silent, because copying the key requires only having
seen the document. This is a property of the carrier, not a claim about any
operator's handling of it.

\paragraph{What a reference buys, and we cannot match it: revocation.} An online
record can be corrected or withdrawn the moment an institution decides, and the
next reader sees the current state. A signature is a statement that was true
when made; it does not become false when the issuer changes their mind, and
nothing in a payload can carry news that postdates it.

Our design does less here than we had implied. The trust list revokes
\emph{keys}, and a revoked key invalidates everything its holder ever signed ---
right for a compromised key, useless for one withdrawn diploma among four
hundred thousand. There is no per-credential revocation in this specification,
and adding one honestly means adding a live service, which is the dependency the
offline design exists to remove. What we do instead is refuse to paper over it:
the result carries a \code{credentialStatus} field whose only value is
\code{unchecked}, present rather than omitted because an absent field reads as
nothing to worry about. For a credential that can be withdrawn and must be
checked without a network, no signature scheme --- ours included --- gives the
reader what a lookup gives them.

\paragraph{Both designs meet the same wall, and that is the useful finding.}
Resolving such a URL produces neither the original document nor a verdict about
the paper in the reader's hand. It produces a short field list --- issuing
institution, subject name, date of birth, document number, award, field of
study, examination date --- above an instruction, in Khmer, telling the reader to
check their document against the data shown.

That instruction is the argument of Section~\ref{sec:transplant}, reached
independently and stated to the public in plain language. Two designs with
opposite architectures arrive at the same irreducible requirement: somebody must
compare the attested fields against the artefact, because no infrastructure on
either side of that step can perform it. What differs is where the requirement
is enforced. A sentence above a table asks a reader who may not read it. A
return type a caller cannot extract a verdict from without handling the fields,
with a test that fails the build if anyone adds a way around it, asks a
programmer who cannot decline. Neither makes a person compare; only one stops
the program pretending the comparison happened. We take that as the transferable
result, and it is why the same discipline appears in Profile~A's payee
disclosure.

The corollary is that no printed mark can be the control either. A certificate
carrying a verification service's logo carries a statement about itself,
reproducible by anyone with a printer; a forgery can bear a convincing badge and
any code its maker likes. The badge advertises a check and will sometimes be
mistaken for one.

\paragraph{What we propose.} Not a replacement. The two compose: a platform that
already holds the authoritative record could emit a signed, offline-verifiable
credential alongside the lookup code at no cost to the lookup path, gaining
archival longevity, offline operation and reader privacy, and reporting three
states rather than two --- current, withdrawn, or \emph{signature valid,
standing unknown}. The design in this paper is a specification for that second
artefact, and the rest of this section returns to it.

\subsection{Trust hierarchy and the fail-closed verifier}

Keys are identified by the first eight bytes of SHA-256 over the uncompressed
public point. The identifier is a lookup hint and never an authenticator: a
verifier encountering two trust-list entries with the same identifier must try
both and accept only on a signature that verifies.

The trust list is signed by an offline Root and carries a monotonic version and
an expiry. A verifier must reject a list numbered below the one it holds
(rollback), must stop verifying once its cached copy exceeds thirty days
(staleness), and must reject when the freshest \emph{timestamp statement} has
expired.

The timestamp statement is a separate signed object with a seven-day validity,
naming the current trust list's version and digest. It exists because expiry
alone does not defend against an attacker who can withhold updates: a hostile
network can pin a verifier to an old but still-valid list, so that a key revoked
yesterday keeps verifying. This is TUF's timestamp role, adopted for the same
reason TUF has it and cited as such \citep{tufspec}. The verifier fails closed:
absent a fresh timestamp statement it stops verifying rather than falling back
on what it holds.

Verification performs no network access. Everything it needs is supplied by the
caller. A verifier that fetched during verification could be stalled or steered
by whoever controls the network at the moment of payment, who at a market stall
is not a trustworthy party. The repository asserts this with a test that
poisons every network global and verifies both reference payloads successfully.

\subsection{No signing key at the edge}

The serving infrastructure holds no private key of any kind. The Root signs
offline in a ceremony; issuer keys live in each institution's hardware security
module; the timestamp statement is produced by a signer outside the serving
infrastructure and uploaded. The registry service accepts certificate signing
requests and queues them; it cannot issue.

This is specification rather than caution: certificate issuance must be
impossible through compromise of the online portal. Because the property is
invisible in the type system and easy to lose in a refactor, it is enforced by a
continuous-integration check that fails if a signing key appears in any service
configuration or source, and we verified that the check fails on a deliberately
introduced violation rather than merely passing on the current tree.

\section{Implementation and measurement}
\label{sec:implementation}

The reference implementation is TypeScript in strict mode, using the Web
Cryptography API exclusively \citep{webcrypto}, with the services deployed as
Cloudflare Workers. It is MIT licensed \citep{khsqr2026repo}.

\subsection{Dependency isolation}

Profile A's transitive import graph reaches seven modules and depends on nothing
beyond Web Crypto: no CBOR, no stream APIs, no packages. The intent is
embeddability --- a mobile wallet should be able to take payment verification and
nothing else, with no polyfill. Profile B additionally uses CBOR and
\code{DecompressionStream}, which is acceptable because Profile B verifiers are
institutional (desktop and web) rather than consumer handsets. A build check
enforces the split and, like the key check, was verified to fail on an
introduced violation.

The CBOR codec is hand-written and covers only the fixed claim shapes in use. A
general CBOR library is a large audit surface on a security-critical path for no
benefit at this payload complexity. The decoder is deliberately strict ---
definite lengths only, minimal integer encodings only, no floating point, no
duplicate map keys, no trailing bytes --- because every construct accepted is a
construct an attacker may use to make two parsers disagree about the same bytes.
It is differentially fuzzed against an independent implementation in continuous
integration.

\subsection{Conformance suite}

The conformance suite is the artefact we expect to outlast the TypeScript. It is
a single JSON file of forty-four cases, thirty-three of them negative and
spanning both container encodings, each carrying an
identifier, a profile, a case type, an input, the trust state and time at which
it is evaluated, the expected outcome and, for a rejection, the expected
machine-readable reason. A Kotlin or Swift port can demonstrate conformance
without reading any of our code.

The negative cases are the substance. An implementation that accepts a
well-formed payload has demonstrated very little; one that rejects a
DER-encoded signature, a data object appended after the signature template, a
sub-tag appended after the signature, a byte mutated inside the signed region, a
byte mutated \emph{outside} it, an expired dynamic code, a static code carrying
an amount, an unknown key identifier, a revoked key, a rolled-back trust list, a
trust list past its cache age, an expired timestamp statement, an
\code{https} payload and a \code{deflate-raw} compression --- each for the correct
stated reason --- has demonstrated most of the specification.

Two case types are distinguished. A \emph{verify} case asserts that a recorded
payload produces a stated outcome. A \emph{roundtrip} case asserts that the
implementation can verify its own output. The distinction is necessary because
neither ECDSA nor DEFLATE is canonical: signing the same input twice yields
different signatures, and two conforming compressors produce different bytes. We
therefore require that the published Profile B payload \emph{decodes and
verifies}, not that an encoder reproduces it.

\subsection{The container was not legacy-transparent, and now is}
\label{sec:container}

The design's premise for using the unreserved template range was that existing
applications would ignore the added template while conforming ones required it.
\textbf{We report that this premise did not hold for a template of this size.}
The first encoding deviated from EMVCo in two respects, and a reviewer was
right that an erratum paragraph was the wrong response to it.

First, EMVCo length fields are exactly two decimal digits with a maximum value
of 99 \citep{emvco2017mpm}. Template \code{85} carries 201 characters and
sub-tag \code{99} carries 128, so both lengths were declared in three digits. A
strict legacy parser reading tag \code{85} interprets the first two of those
digits as the length, consumes 20 characters, misparses everything following,
fails to find the CRC tag, and rejects the payload outright --- rather than
ignoring the template as intended. Second, EMVCo requires an unreserved
template to carry a Globally Unique Identifier at sub-tag \code{00}
\citep{emvco2017mpm}; version~1 used \code{00} for a format version, so the
template did not identify its own namespace.

Neither deviation is a security defect --- the length characters lie inside the
signed prefix, so tampering with them invalidates the signature, and the
verifier locates the signature by position rather than by any length field ---
but the deployment consequence is real and was absent from the original
reasoning. Cross-border acceptance makes it worse: a foreign wallet
implementing EMVCo and nothing else has no reason to know about this scheme,
and is exactly the parser that would reject the payload.

\paragraph{Encoding version 2.} We have therefore specified and implemented a
second encoding that stays inside EMVCo. Every length field is two digits with
a value of at most 99; each unreserved template carries the scheme's identifier
at sub-tag \code{00}; and the signature is split at 64 characters across
templates \code{86} and \code{87}, because 128 characters cannot fit in one
template whatever else is done. Templates \code{85}, \code{86} and \code{87}
must be the final three data objects before the CRC, which is what prevents an
attacker appending data while the signed prefix stays byte-identical.

The property that mattered survives. The signed region is still a plain
substring of the received payload, never a re-serialisation of parsed fields,
so there is still no canonical form to disagree about. What moves is where the
prefix ends: version~1 ended at the fixed marker \code{99128}; version~2 ends
where template \code{86} begins, which the same TLV walk a legacy parser
performs locates exactly. Because every length is now valid, that walk succeeds
for both parties, which is the whole point.

\paragraph{What it costs.} The reference dynamic payload grows from 317 to 381
characters, and the symbol from QR version~10 to version~11 --- $57\times57$ to
$61\times61$ modules at error-correction level~M (Table~\ref{tab:qr}). The cost
is three repeated identifiers and one extra template header. We think that is
the right trade: a symbol one version larger is a printing cost, while a payload
a conforming parser cannot read is a migration the scheme cannot complete.

Version~1 is frozen and deprecated for new issuance rather than withdrawn. Its
vectors are published and cited, and codes already printed must keep verifying;
the specification requires a verifier to support both and to dispatch on the
encoding rather than attempt either with the other's rules. The conformance
suite carries four version~2 cases --- two accepting, and two rejecting the
attacks the new structure introduces, namely data appended after the signature
templates and a foreign identifier in place of the scheme's.

We also record an erratum in the version~1 vectors: the reference payload
declares template \code{85}'s length as 200 where its content is 201
characters. It is retained unchanged as a verification vector, since its
signature is valid and a conforming verifier accepts it.

\subsection{Mirror independence is not satisfied}
\label{sec:mirrors}

The specification requires the trust list to be published at three mirrors under
\emph{distinct operational control}. Our reference deployment places all three
behind one provider.

Anycast delivers availability, not operational independence. Three URLs on one
provider share one account, one contractual relationship, one legal
jurisdiction, and one governance failure. If that account is suspended,
compromised, or subjected to a legal order, all three mirrors fail together, and
the verifier population enters its fail-closed staleness path at once.

\textbf{We therefore do not claim conformance to this clause}, and the service's
own health endpoint reports that it does not. Meeting it requires at least two
mirrors elsewhere --- the NBC's own infrastructure and a second commercial
provider are the obvious candidates. We state this because a specification that
requires operational independence and an implementation that quietly does not
provide it is precisely the kind of gap that a deployment inherits without
noticing.

\section{What the design does not achieve}
\label{sec:notachieved}

This is the most important section of the paper. For each item we give the
reason it is unreachable from the code layer, not merely the observation that it
is.

\subsection{Authorised push payment fraud}

A criminal shows a victim a genuine KHQR --- their own, correctly registered,
correctly signed --- with a false story: a refund to be released, a parcel to be
cleared, a fee to be paid. The victim scans, reads the payee name, and
authorises.

\textbf{Why it is unreachable.} Every byte of the payload is authentic, and a
correct implementation of this specification verifies it. There is no forgery to
detect because nothing was forged. The falsehood exists in the sentence spoken
or printed above the code, which is not part of the payload, is not available to
the verifier, and could not be signed by anyone even in principle --- there is no
party in a position to attest to the truth of an arbitrary claim about why a
payment is owed.

This is the layer boundary of Section~\ref{sec:layers} in its sharpest form. The
control is enforced on the instrument; the deception is created in the
interaction. One cannot govern the other because they are not the same kind of
object.

\paragraph{The pretext need not be about the payment at all.}
The variant most often described in Cambodian reporting does not lie about why
money is owed; it lies about who is asking. An account belonging to a relative
or friend is taken over, the contact list is worked through in turn, and each
person is asked for urgent help by someone they have every reason to
trust; a QR code is supplied to receive the money, and reporting notes that the
code presented resembles the account holder's own \citep{kiripost2024scams}.

This is worth separating because it defeats the one mitigation we offer against
this category. Surfacing the payee name to the payer assumes the payer has an
expectation to compare it against. Here the expectation is supplied by the
attacker and is wrong: a payer helping a relative in trouble may have never seen
that relative's payee string, may expect a name they cannot spell in Latin
script, or may reasonably accept a near-match as the relative's own account. The
verifier is entirely correct, the display is entirely correct, and the control
still does nothing, because the payer's model of who they are paying was
constructed before the code was ever scanned. We record this as a limit on the
mitigation, not as a limit on the evidence.

\paragraph{One subset of it is reachable, and the difference is not in the lie.}
This category divides on a property that has nothing to do with the deception:
whether the payee account is presented to one victim or to many. A pretext built
for a single victim --- a specific parcel, a particular refund --- consumes the
account and abandons it, and no register will ever be populated while it still
matters. A pretext that is a standing offer --- goods advertised and never sent,
a service sold and never delivered --- must present the same payee to every
victim it recruits, because collecting to a fresh account for each victim costs
the operator an identity, and identities are the scarce input. The second kind
therefore accumulates reports against a stable identifier \emph{while it is
still operating}, which is the only condition under which a risk list does
anything at all.

Nothing in the payload distinguishes the two, and no verifier can: both are
genuine codes presented with a lie. The distinction is visible only in
aggregate, across victims, after the fact --- which is to say it is visible to an
institution and not to a payer. This is why we claim the screening layer of
Section~\ref{sec:screening} reaches part of this category rather than none of
it, and it is also the reason time-to-list (Section~\ref{sec:ttl}) is the metric
that matters rather than detection accuracy: the reachable subset is reachable
only inside the window in which the account is still being reused. We make no
claim about how large that subset is in Cambodia, for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:data}.

The design's only honest response is interface discipline: surfacing the payee
to the payer, and refusing to let a verification result be reduced to a tick.
Our specification makes both normative. We do not claim they are sufficient, and
Section~\ref{sec:indicators} explains why we expect them to be less effective
than they appear.

\subsection{The authentic code that means something else}
\label{sec:currency}

In October 2025 the Ministry of Interior's Department of Countering Technology
Crime arrested a tuk-tuk driver in Phnom Penh who had presented foreign
passengers with QR codes for their fares. The code was his. The account was his,
correctly registered. The amount was the number the parties had agreed. The
currency was not: a fare agreed at 7,200 riel was collected as 7,200 US dollars
\citep{khmertimes2025qrcurrency}.

We had not enumerated this case, and it is not any of the four already listed.
It is not forgery, because nothing was forged. It is not authorised push payment
fraud in the ordinary sense, because the pretext was true --- there really was a
fare, owed to really that driver, who really did provide the ride. It is not
registration abuse, because the account was a legitimate account being used for
its actual purpose. Every field in the payee disclosure is correct, including
the merchant name that our interface obligations exist to surface. The deception
is carried entirely by tag~\texttt{53}, three characters wide, and by the payer's
assumption about what the number beside it meant.

\paragraph{The signature makes it worse.} This is the sharpest instance in this
paper of its own thesis, and it inverts the usual direction of the argument.
Elsewhere we say a signature does not help against deception. Here it assists
it. The amount and the currency lie inside the signed prefix, so a conforming
verifier confirms them both; an interface that reduces the result to a tick has
then presented the wrong number to the payer as \emph{checked}. Authentication
applied to a field the payer does not read does not leave them where they
started. It moves them backwards.

\paragraph{Dollarisation is what makes it expensive.} A currency confusion in a
single-currency economy is a rounding error. Cambodia circulates riel and US
dollars together at a rate of roughly four thousand to one --- ACLEDA Bank
published a buying rate of 4{,}042 riel to the dollar on the day this section was
written \citep{acleda2026tips} --- so the same three characters carry a factor of
four thousand.

That both currencies are live on the same rail is not an inference. Of the
Bakong transactions the NBC reports for 2025, 771.2 million were in riel and
554.5 million in US dollars \citep{nbc2025annual}: a split of roughly 58 to 42.
Tag~\texttt{53} is therefore not a field that is almost always the same value
and occasionally otherwise --- it is a field that genuinely varies, on a rail
where getting it wrong costs three orders of magnitude and change. The vulnerability is not in the payload format, which is EMVCo's
and is correct. It is in the substrate, in exactly the sense of
Section~\ref{sec:degrees}: a property of the monetary environment that a
designer does not choose but must design around.

\paragraph{What we changed, and what we did not.} The specification previously
required the payee disclosure to be \emph{available} to the interface and only
recommended displaying it. That was too weak for this field, so amount and
currency together are now a MUST before authorisation, the currency must be
shown as an alphabetic code or an unambiguous name, and the numeric code from
tag~\texttt{53} must never be shown to a human. The library now resolves the
numeric code to an alphabetic one and returns \texttt{null} rather than guessing
when it cannot, so that an interface which cannot name the currency is obliged
to say so instead of implying the local one.

That the field is genuinely overlooked rather than merely overlookable has
independent support. Cambodia's largest commercial bank tells customers, in its
own published security guidance, to ``always verify the merchant's name on your
screen before authorizing a payment'' \citep{acleda2026tips}. That is the right
instruction, and it names the one field in this attack that was never wrong.
Currency is not mentioned.

We do not claim the change fixes the case. It obliges an interface to show the
currency; it cannot oblige a passenger to read it, and a passenger who has
agreed a price out loud has already formed the expectation that the display
would have to overturn. What the change does is remove our excuse. The prior
specification permitted a conforming wallet in which this attack was invisible.

\subsection{Registration abuse}
\label{sec:registration}

A fraudster registers a merchant account using genuine documents --- their own, or
a coerced or purchased identity --- and receives a genuine key. The codes they
produce are correctly signed by a registered issuer, because that is exactly
what they are.

\textbf{Why it is unreachable.} The signature attests to an identity that is
real. Its truthfulness is not in question; its \emph{legitimacy} is, and
legitimacy is a fact about intent and history rather than about bytes. No
property computable from the payload distinguishes a genuine merchant from a
genuine criminal who completed the same registration process. The defence is
know-your-customer quality and post-hoc detection, both of which live at layer~3
and neither of which is cryptography.

\textbf{And the identity is more often borrowed than bought.} We wrote
``coerced or purchased'' above because that is the standard framing. The
Cambodian case discussed in Section~\ref{sec:integrity} suggests a more ordinary
mechanism: collection accounts opened in the names of a brother, a son, a
daughter and an associate, alongside two national identity cards held by the
principal \citep{cambodianess2026kohkong}. Those are real people with real
documents presenting themselves in person. No onboarding check separates a
genuine relative from a genuine relative acting as a nominee, because at the
moment of registration there is nothing to separate. What distinguishes them is
the later pattern of use, which is visible across institutions and not within
one --- so the burden falls back on dissemination
(Section~\ref{sec:ttl}) rather than on the registration desk.

\subsection{Counterfeit verifiers}
\label{sec:counterfeit}
\label{sec:indicators}

An application that shows a green tick for every code defeats the entire scheme
without breaking any cryptography. An application trust list helps a diligent
user check what they installed and does nothing for a user who installed the
application from a link in a message.

\textbf{Why it is unreachable, and why security indicators disappoint.} The
relevant empirical result is Schechter et al.'s evaluation of website
authentication indicators \citep{schechter2007emperor}. In a study of 67 bank
customers, among the 25 participants who used their own accounts, 23 --- 92 per
cent --- entered their passwords after the site-authentication image, which they
had previously selected and were expected to verify, was removed and replaced
with an upgrade message.

We cite this precisely because it is the closest well-controlled evidence to the
question at hand, and we note its limits: it studied website images rather than
QR verification, in 2007, in a different population. It does not transfer
automatically to Cambodian merchants in 2026. What it establishes is narrower
and still decisive for design purposes: the \emph{absence} of a positive
security indicator is not reliably noticed by users who have been trained to
expect it. A scheme whose safety property is ``the user will notice when the
tick is missing'' rests on an assumption that has been tested and did not hold.

This is why the design excludes URL carriers rather than displaying domains
prominently, and why the credential API refuses to expose a boolean. Both are
attempts to avoid depending on a user noticing an absence.

\subsection{Foreign and platform codes}
\label{sec:foreign}

Codes issued by systems outside the national trust hierarchy --- foreign wallets,
international platforms, ticketing and loyalty systems --- cannot be verified.

\textbf{Why it is unreachable, and why it is worse than it looks.} This is not
merely a coverage gap; it actively damages layers~1 and~2. A verifier that
displays ``unverified'' for the majority of codes a person encounters trains
that person to proceed past the warning. The indicator's value decays in
proportion to how often it fires benignly, which is the same mechanism that made
browser certificate warnings ineffective. A national scheme that verifies only
domestic codes may therefore leave users \emph{less} attentive than one that
makes no claim at all --- a possibility we flag rather than resolve, since we have
no data on it.

\paragraph{This is current, not prospective, and it runs both ways.} KHQR is
already linked to counterpart schemes in the region, so inbound foreign codes
are not a future scenario: a merchant near a border or in a tourist district
encounters them as ordinary traffic. A domestic-only trust list combined with
inbound cross-border acceptance is therefore not a gap waiting to open. It is an
indicator-decay machine already running, and the more successful the linkages
are, the faster it runs.

The outbound direction is the one worth separating, because
Section~\ref{sec:container} can be misread as having solved it. Encoding
version~2 makes a KH-SQR payload \emph{parseable} by a foreign wallet
implementing EMVCo and nothing else: it walks the payload, reaches the CRC, and
correctly ignores templates \code{85}--\code{87} as unreserved. That is exactly
what we claimed and it is worth having. It is not verification. The foreign
wallet holds no Cambodian trust list, cannot resolve the key identifier, and so
pays against an unchecked signature --- while displaying nothing wrong, because
from its point of view nothing is wrong.

Cross-scheme linkage sharpens this rather than softening it. Where two schemes
interoperate through an acquirer and a switching operator
(Section~\ref{sec:japan}), the assurances that exist run between those
intermediaries. They are not carried in the payload, not checkable by the
payer's wallet, and not visible to the payer at all --- so the payer's evidence
about a cross-border payment is strictly weaker than about a domestic one, at
the moment when they are furthest from any recourse.

Legibility and verifiability are different properties and only the first is a
wire-format question. The second needs trust-list federation between schemes,
which this work does not attempt and which our Limitations place out of scope.
Stating it plainly matters because the alternative reading --- that an
EMVCo-conformant encoding extends the signature's protection across a border ---
is both attractive and false.

\subsection{Device compromise, and the assumption verification makes about the verifier}
\label{sec:device}

Every interface obligation in Section~\ref{sec:notachieved} assumes something the
specification never states: that the payer's handset shows the payer what the
verifier computed. Threat research published in April 2026 by Infoblox Threat
Intel with the Vietnamese non-profit Chong Lua Dao documents why that assumption
should not be granted \citep{infoblox2026cambodia}. The researchers traced an
Android banking trojan, distributed through a malware-as-a-service platform and
assessed as operated at least in part from the K99 Triumph City compound in
Sihanoukville, from a spike in DNS queries across their customer networks. The
operation registers on the order of thirty-five new domains a month,
impersonating banks, pension funds, revenue authorities, utilities, immigration
and police services across at least twenty-one countries.

The attack chain matters here more than the malware. Operators contact targets
by voice over IP while impersonating government officials, move the conversation
to a messaging application, and send \textbf{a link or a QR code} pointing at a
lure page. They then talk the victim through installing the application and
granting permissions, explicitly coaching them past the operating system's own
warnings. Once installed, the operator intercepts the SMS one-time passcode and
presents a counterfeit know-your-customer overlay that induces the victim to
perform a facial-recognition check --- which authenticates the \emph{operator}
into the victim's banking application. The victim believes throughout that they
are complying with a new government programme.

Three consequences follow for this design, and the third is the one we should
have written down before seeing this report.

\paragraph{Coached installation defeats every passive defence at layer~2.}
Section~\ref{sec:indicators} treats a counterfeit verifier as a user who chose
badly, and offers an application trust list against it. This is a human
adversary walking the victim through the choice in real time, with rapport
already established by telephone, instructing them to disregard exactly the
warnings a trust list would rely on. No curation of an application store reaches
a user who is being talked past the warnings by someone they believe is an
official.

\paragraph{Consent is harvestable.}
The biometric step is not stolen; it is performed by the victim, willingly, under
a false description of what it is for. This is authorised push payment fraud's
logic applied to authentication rather than to payment: the control operated
correctly, the user genuinely consented, and the consent meant something other
than what the user thought.

\paragraph{Verification assumes an uncompromised verifier, and we did not say so.}
Section~\ref{sec:design} is careful that verification requires no network, and
gives the reason. It is silent on requiring an uncompromised device, and that
silence is a defect. On a handset under an operator's control the payee
disclosure that Section~\ref{sec:notachieved} obliges an interface to show can be
suppressed, altered, or overlaid. Every user-facing guarantee in this
specification is conditional on the integrity of the device presenting it, and
that condition is under documented, industrial-scale attack from infrastructure
hosted in the country this design is for.

\paragraph{What this evidence does and does not show.}
It is not evidence about the prevalence of QR fraud in Cambodia, and we do not
use it as such: Section~\ref{sec:data}'s finding stands unchanged. The documented
attack is account takeover --- unauthorised fraud --- rather than the authorised
push payment fraud of Section~\ref{sec:notachieved}, and its victims are
largely outside Cambodia, with the highest query volumes observed in Indonesia,
Thailand, Spain and T\"urkiye. Cambodia appears here as a host of the
infrastructure rather than as the principal market for it. What the report
establishes is narrower and sufficient for our purposes: QR codes are in
operational use as a delivery vector for device compromise, the technique
defeats device-side verification, and the operators run it at industrial scale
using, on the researchers' account and that of the United Nations reporting they
cite, forced labour.

\subsection{The scan path: verification the user never invokes}
\label{sec:scanpath}

Every control in Section~\ref{sec:design} runs inside a conforming wallet. None
of it runs when the code is scanned with the handset's native camera
application, which is how a great many codes are in fact scanned --- a camera is
already open, it recognises the square, and it offers to act on it.

\textbf{Why it is unreachable.} The verifier cannot intervene in a scan it is
never asked to perform. The exclusion of URL carriers
(Section~\ref{sec:design}) is the clearest case: the rule is sound and the
implementation enforces it, but a URL-bearing code scanned by the camera
application opens in a browser without ever reaching any code we wrote. Closing
this in software would require the operating-system vendors to ship a
Cambodia-specific rule, which is not within national control and would not reach
the installed base of inexpensive handsets in any case.

This is the residual risk that motivates
Section~\ref{sec:categorical}, and it is why that section's intervention is not
software.

\subsection{The gap widens, and this design widens part of it}
\label{sec:trajectory}

Everything above is stated as of now. The more uncomfortable claim is about
direction.

Section~\ref{sec:background} described two boundaries --- what the authorities
actually control, and what the public extends its trust to --- and presented the
distance between them as a static gap. It is not static. The two grow at
different rates, and only one of them can contract.

The \textbf{trust boundary} grows linearly and is bounded by administrative
capacity: one issuer registered, one code signed, one credential type covered at
a time. The \textbf{credulity boundary} grows with public habituation, and does
not contract. Five mechanisms drive it, each running one way only:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Late adopters inherit the QR code as furniture.} Early users
    encountered it as a novel instrument and learned some caution with it. Every
    subsequent cohort receives it already normalised, and learns none.
  \item \textbf{Every uneventful scan strengthens the prior.} The base rate of
    safe scans is overwhelming, and remains overwhelming even as fraud rises, so
    ordinary experience keeps confirming that scanning is safe. Individual
    evidence and population risk move in opposite directions.
  \item \textbf{Attacker learning is cumulative and shared; victim learning is
    neither.} A pretext that works becomes a template and propagates. Having
    been defrauded propagates poorly, because shame suppresses the telling.
  \item \textbf{The value at risk per account rises} as salary, bills and
    remittances migrate onto the rail. The same attack yields more for no
    additional effort.
  \item \textbf{The substrate does not improve on its own.} Finality and the
    liquidity of the output (Section~\ref{sec:japan}) are fixed unless
    deliberately changed, so the loss per successful incident stays at its
    maximum.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The same trajectory is now running on documents, one step behind.}
Everything above concerns payments, because that is where habituation is
complete. Document verification is at the stage payments were at earlier: a
national platform exists, adoption is real but the artefacts are not yet
load-bearing in daily life, and the payoff to attacking it is correspondingly
low. That is a statement about \emph{today}, and the mechanisms listed above do
not care which artefact they run on.

The attack the trajectory produces is worth stating before it arrives, because
its shape is already fully determined by the design. A forged certificate, land
title or official notice is manufactured with a QR code of the forger's
choosing. The recipient is told the document is genuine and invited to check it
--- \emph{scan the code and see for yourself} --- and the code opens a
counterfeit page that confirms the document and, in the second stage, asks the
reader to enter their own details to complete the check. Every element is
supplied by the attacker: the paper, the code, the page, and the instruction to
scan.

Three things make this worse than its payment equivalent rather than merely
analogous. There is no genuine artefact underneath, as there is beneath a
sticker on a merchant's code, so nothing is displaced and nothing goes missing
to be noticed later. The invitation to verify is not a red flag but the
mechanism working as designed --- a forger who says ``scan it and check''
sounds exactly like an honest issuer, and confidence in the document rises
because the check was offered. And the reader's only defence is reading a domain
name, since a counterfeit page is reached the same way the real one is, which
returns to Section~\ref{sec:indicators}.

The residue is what makes the timing matter. Habituation is what the attack
consumes, and habituation is what successful adoption produces: the more
reliably a state platform teaches the public that scanning a government code
yields an authoritative answer, the more a counterfeit of it is worth. An
offline signature removes the page from the attack entirely --- there is no
service to impersonate when the verifier consults nothing --- and relocates the
residue to the verifier application, which Section~\ref{sec:counterfeit} says is
addressed weakly. That is a smaller and better-understood problem than a
counterfeit website, and it is the case for making the change before the
platform is load-bearing rather than after, when the installed base of printed
documents will make it expensive.

\paragraph{And this design widens the gap it cannot close.}
Every signed code that a person scans successfully teaches them that the system
checks these codes on their behalf. That raises credulity. It does not extend
the trust boundary over what actually harms them, because
Section~\ref{sec:notachieved} is untouched by any of it. A measure that
manufactures precisely the confidence the dominant attack feeds on is not a
neutral addition to the system.

We therefore state the expected effect plainly. Deploying this design should be
expected to reduce forgery \emph{and} to increase exposure to authorised push
payment fraud. We do not know the sign of the sum. This is the result of
Section~\ref{sec:indicators} in dynamic form: users do not reliably notice an
indicator that is absent, and they do generalise from one that is present.

\paragraph{The trajectory is not observable.}
With no baseline there is no trend. A country without incident statistics cannot
detect a gradual rise in fraud it does not measure, and the first reliable signal
is a crisis rather than a gradient. Section~\ref{sec:data} argues for mandatory
reporting on the ground that prioritisation cannot otherwise be argued from
evidence; this is the stronger form of that argument. Without measurement, the
trajectory described here cannot be observed even in principle, and the net
effect of deploying this design will remain unknown for as long as that holds.

There is a governance consequence, and it is the more serious half. A commercial
system in this position would at least be told: it faces competitors, it loses
share, and the loss is legible early enough to force a decision while the
decision is still cheap. National payment and credential infrastructure faces no
competitor and offers its users no exit, so no such signal is generated. Without
published incident statistics there is no scoreboard either, and a system can
therefore degrade for years with nothing that would compel anyone to act ---
while the installed base of printed artefacts, registered merchants and issued
credentials grows monotonically, so the cost of changing course rises the whole
time. Measurement is not only how prioritisation is argued from evidence. It is
the only mechanism available here that could produce pressure to change a format
while changing it is still affordable.

\paragraph{What follows for sequencing.}
If exposure grows, the interventions of Section~\ref{sec:institutional} do not
age equally, and the difference is systematic.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Detection-dependent controls degrade with volume.} Screening
    protects nobody until somebody has listed the account; the contest procedure
    requires officers to answer within a deadline. Both consume institutional
    effort in proportion to attacker volume, and in a rising market both lose
    that race.
  \item \textbf{Substrate controls are volume-independent.} Exit controls
    (Section~\ref{sec:exit}) do not care how many attacks occur. Illiquidity
    applies identically at ten incidents and at ten thousand, and costs no more
    to enforce at either.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Under a rising trajectory the ordering should therefore shift further
towards the substrate and away from detection. We record this as a consequence
of the argument rather than as a recommendation we can support with measurement.

\paragraph{Who ends up bearing it, if nothing at the substrate changes.}
One consequence of a rising trajectory is distributional, and it is already
visible rather than projected. Where a rail supplies no reversibility, somebody
sells it: Section~\ref{sec:degrees} describes a Cambodian firm that interposes
itself between buyers and overseas sellers and guarantees against non-delivery,
which is an escrow, priced per transaction.

That is a market answering a demand the substrate declines to meet, and its
incidence is regressive by construction. Reversibility bought privately reaches
whoever can pay for it and transacts through an intermediary that offers it. It
does not reach the market seller taking a domestic KHQR payment, the tuk-tuk
driver, or the micro-merchant for whom the fee is a material fraction of the
margin --- who are also the people least able to absorb an irrecoverable loss,
and who therefore carry the unmitigated irrevocability that better-resourced
users have bought their way out of.

If the interventions of Section~\ref{sec:institutional} are not taken and the
trajectory continues, this is the equilibrium: safety becomes a product rather
than a property of the rail, and the distribution of exposure inverts the
distribution of capacity to bear it. A substrate-level control has the opposite
incidence, because it applies to everyone on the rail at once and cannot be
bought out of. That is a further reason for the sequencing above, and it is an
argument from structure --- we have no data on how many Cambodians already pay
for intermediated safety, and we do not claim to.

\paragraph{What kind of claim this is.}
It is a mechanism argument, not a forecast. Each item above is structural and
one-directional, and we can say which way the gap moves. We cannot say how fast,
and with no incident data we can neither estimate the magnitude nor, at present,
observe it. We make no quantitative prediction, and we would distrust one.

\subsection{Summary}

Layers~1 and~2 are the parts of the problem that cryptography addresses, and
this design addresses them. Layers~3 and~4 are the parts that matter most in
current Cambodian fraud, and it does not address them at all. Any presentation
of this work that inverts that emphasis misrepresents it. And by
Section~\ref{sec:trajectory}, the share of the problem it does not address
should be expected to grow --- partly for reasons that have nothing to do with
this design, and partly because of it.

There is, however, a constructive corollary that the layer argument itself
supplies, and it is the subject of Section~\ref{sec:categorical}. If the harm is
created above the enforcement point, the response is to raise the enforcement
point rather than to strengthen the control beneath it. The layer above the code
is not empty: it contains the rules governing what legitimate issuers are
permitted to emit. A control placed there does not authenticate the illegitimate
set; it empties the legitimate one, until anything outside becomes recognisable
without authentication.

\section{The institutional layer}
\label{sec:institutional}

Layers~3 and~4 are not reachable from the code, but they are not unreachable
altogether. They are reachable from the institutions, by two mechanisms that are
not cryptography: constraining what legitimate issuers are permitted to emit,
and sharing information about accounts. We take them in that order, because the
first is the cheaper and the more decisive.

\subsection{The response so far, and the signal that was never built}
\label{sec:response}

Cambodian authorities identified this threat early. On 27 January 2024 the
Department of Technology and Crime Prevention issued a public warning about
``quishing'' and phishing by QR code, naming three harms: users tricked into
reaching fake websites, users induced to download infected software, and
\emph{impersonation of a payee} \citep{khmertimes2024quishing}. The last is the
overlay attack of Section~\ref{sec:layers}, recognised officially two years
before this paper was written. Nothing here is a threat the authorities have
failed to notice.

The warning asked citizens to do three things: check the source and look for
anomalies in the QR code's design before scanning; beware of fake links,
particularly redirect links and link shorteners; and share the advice onward.
Public-awareness material issued since has repeated the same category of
guidance.

Three observations follow, none of which requires knowing whether any campaign
changed an outcome.

\paragraph{Every ask is directed at the public.}
None of the three binds an institution to do, or to refrain from, anything. The
third is explicitly to propagate the advice further. The burden of detection is
placed entirely on the party with the least information, the least time and the
least expertise, at the moment of payment.

\paragraph{Two of the three ask for judgements the medium does not support.}
``Check for anomalies in the QR code design'' asks a person to visually assess a
field of black and white modules for irregularity. Outside the case of a
physically visible sticker edge there is nothing in the symbology for an unaided
reader to assess, and at an unfamiliar merchant there is no reference image to
compare against. ``Beware of link shorteners'' asks a reader to be wary of a
construct whose defining property is that the destination is not visible: the
evaluation the advice calls for is precisely the one the construct exists to
prevent. These are not poorly worded instructions. They are instructions for a
task that cannot be performed.

\paragraph{The message degrades as it travels, and quickly.}
Ten days after the warning, a national newspaper published a follow-up
explaining the threat to a business readership, drawing on named
industry and academic sources \citep{khmertimes2024counter}. It defines quishing
as ``a form of phishing that specifically utilizes phone calls or voice
communications''. That is vishing. Quishing is phishing carried by a QR code,
which is the subject of both the warning and the article containing the
sentence.

We note this without any interest in the individuals quoted, because the point
is not that somebody was wrong. It is that the definition inverted inside ten
days, in print, in the country's English-language business paper, in an article
whose purpose was to explain the threat. A control whose mechanism is public
understanding has to survive that transmission channel, and this is what the
channel does.

The article's remedies are the second half of the observation. They are
encryption, biometric authentication, security audits, strong and unique
passwords, two-factor authentication, updated antivirus software, and manually
typing website addresses instead of clicking links. Every item is sound general
security advice and not one of them touches the mechanism: a QR code carries no
password, and encryption is a confidentiality property, whereas the failure
here --- a sticker over a merchant's code, an unsigned payload, a currency the
payer never sees --- is an \emph{authenticity} failure end to end. Nothing in the
list would have prevented any Cambodian case discussed in this paper. That is
the substantive argument for a control that lives in the payload and in the
verifier, where the property in question actually is.

\paragraph{And the institutions issuing the guidance do not follow it.}
In 2026 the national electricity utility promoted, from its verified social
media account, a facility for customers to scan the QR code printed on their
electricity bill and pay it in the utility's own application
\citep{edc2026qrbill}. The flow itself is unobjectionable and, notably, complies
with the rule proposed below: the scan happens inside a payment application, no
browser opens, and the customer is asked to pay rather than to authenticate.

The promotion is another matter. It closes by inviting readers who do not yet
have the application to install it through a shortened link, served over plain
\code{http}. Both properties --- a link shortener, and an unencrypted scheme ---
are on the Department of Technology and Crime Prevention's own published list of
things a citizen should treat as suspicious \citep{khmertimes2024quishing}. A
member of the public who had absorbed the state's advice and applied it here
would have concluded that a genuine notice from a state utility was a scam.

This is a sharper version of the transmission problem than the definitional
error above, because nothing has been misunderstood. The advice is correct, the
utility's offer is genuine, and the two are simply incompatible. A rule the
public is asked to apply is being falsified, in ordinary official practice, by
the same state that issued it --- which is precisely the decay mechanism
Section~\ref{sec:indicators} describes, arriving from the direction nobody
guards against.

\paragraph{A comparison worth making about where the obligation sits.} Japan
published a guideline in 2020 on preventing the fraudulent linking of bank
accounts to code-payment accounts \citep{jpqrfraud}. It addresses a different
problem from this section's --- account and funding-source abuse rather than
deception at the point of payment --- so we draw no conclusion about relative
effectiveness. What transfers is the \emph{addressee}. Its controls are
obligations on the operator at each stage: what identity information to collect
at account creation, reconciling attributes against the financial institution's
own records, preventing accounts in several names from being linked to one bank
account, capping how many accounts one bank account may be linked to, limiting
top-up and payment amounts, and standing monitoring and incident-response
arrangements.

Not one of them asks the public to be more careful. Section~\ref{sec:categorical}
argues that a rule the public must apply has to be categorical to be usable at
all; this is the complementary observation, that most of the work in a
comparable scheme was not given to the public in the first place. It also bears
on registration abuse (Section~\ref{sec:registration}), which we describe as
reachable only through know-your-customer quality: capping accounts per funding
source and blocking rapid re-linking are structural controls on the same attack,
and they do not depend on an onboarding officer's judgement.

\paragraph{The model has a witness against it.} Every instrument described
above asks the public to be more careful. The clearest evidence we have found
against that model comes from somebody with no interest in making the argument.
Sem Putheary, a computer science graduate with a decade of professional
experience, lost about five hundred dollars to an online purchase fraud and
afterwards founded a company to protect other buyers from it
\citep{wingbank2025espass}. Her own account of it is the point: ``I thought I
was knowledgeable, yet I still fell victim. What about those who aren't? What
will happen to them?''

This is one person and we draw no statistical conclusion from it. What it
establishes is a bound on what the vigilance model can be expected to deliver.
If public education worked as its own theory of change requires, it would have
worked on her: she is at the extreme upper tail of the very variable the
campaigns try to shift. That a countermeasure fails on its best-case subject is
not proof it fails generally, but it does mean the countermeasure cannot be the
primary control, and the question of what carries the load has to be answered
somewhere else. This paper's answer is Sections~\ref{sec:categorical}
to~\ref{sec:liability}: controls that do not require the payer to be right.

\paragraph{The interval matters more than any single campaign.}
Between that warning and the awareness material still being published in
mid-2026 lies about thirty-one months of the same instrument. We draw no
conclusion about its effect, because none is available: no measurement exists
that would tell anyone --- including the departments running the campaigns ---
whether any of it changed anything. Section~\ref{sec:data} argues that the
absence of incident statistics prevents evidence-based prioritisation. This is
the same absence seen from the other side. A government has sustained an
intervention for two and a half years with no feedback signal of any kind, and
that is not a failure of diligence; it is a consequence of never having built
the measurement.

\paragraph{August 2026 is a different kind of step, and it deserves saying so.}
In late August 2026 the National Police launched \emph{Kapea Scams}, a public
application for reporting online and technology-related fraud
\citep{khmertimes2026kapea}. Two features distinguish it from everything above.

First, it contains an actual control rather than advice. Reports feed a
capability to block incoming calls automatically from numbers identified as
linked to scams. That is the first Cambodian measure in this evidence that asks
the citizen to judge nothing: the block is enforced by the system. It acts on
the attacker's channel, which is the right target, and it is subject to the
limit Section~\ref{sec:trajectory} describes for every detection-dependent
control --- a number must be identified before it can be blocked, numbers are
cheap, and the mechanism therefore scales against attacker volume rather than
with it.

Second, and more consequentially, it builds collection. The stated intent is to
analyse submitted reports to identify emerging scam patterns, to drive public
warning messages, and to inform further systems.

\paragraph{What is missing is one sentence, not one system.}
Publication is not among those stated uses. The reported purposes --- police
action, warning messages, internal system development --- are all internal. None
of them produces a public statistic, and a national dataset that is never
published cannot support the evidence-based prioritisation
Section~\ref{sec:data} asks for, nor allow anyone outside the police to
establish whether the trajectory of
Section~\ref{sec:trajectory} is real.

This narrows our first recommendation considerably, and makes it much cheaper.
We are not asking for a reporting system to be built: one now exists. We are
asking for a publication duty to be attached to it, and for the collection to
be extended from victim self-reporting --- which under-reports severely, for the
reasons Section~\ref{sec:trajectory} gives --- to mandatory institutional
reporting by banks and payment institutions, who observe the money movement that
victims do not. The mechanism is new, which is precisely when a publication
obligation is cheapest to attach.

\paragraph{The coordination this paper asks for already exists.}
Section~\ref{sec:categorical} identifies the critical dependency of a
categorical prohibition as an instrument reaching beyond the financial
perimeter, into telecommunications and the line ministries. The evidence above
shows several arms of government already working this problem together: a
crime-prevention department under the interior portfolio, a digital-government
programme under posts and telecommunications, and an anti-cybercrime department
of the National Police operating both enforcement and a public reporting
channel.
The capacity to coordinate is demonstrated. What the next section proposes is
not new machinery but a change of target: the same coordination, pointed at the
few hundred licensed institutions that can be bound, rather than at the
population that cannot act on what it is being told.

\subsection{The categorical rule: a payment QR never opens a website}
\label{sec:categorical}

The URL-bearing QR code --- ``scan to claim your refund'', ``scan to verify your
document'' --- is the one attack in this decomposition that a national authority
can eliminate outright. It is not hypothetical: the delivery step documented in
Section~\ref{sec:device} is precisely ``a link or a QR code'' sent to the victim
by an operator impersonating an official \citep{infoblox2026cambodia}. Note that
the rule proposed here reaches that case as readily as it reaches a sticker on a
wall, because it is stated in terms of what the victim observes rather than
where the code was found --- and note that ``check the source before scanning''
does not reach it at all, the source being a government official the victim has
already spoken to by telephone. It cannot be eliminated in software, for the reason
given in Section~\ref{sec:scanpath}: the code is frequently scanned by an
application that will never consult a trust list. It can be eliminated by rule.

\paragraph{The rule has two asymmetric halves.}
The asymmetry is the mechanism, and it is deliberate: the population that bears
the rule and the population that bears the obligation are different, and each is
given the form of instruction it can actually follow.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Public rule.} A QR code never opens a website. If a website opens, it is
a scam: do not pay, and do not enter your PIN, password, one-time code or
personal details.
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Institutional prohibition.} No licensed bank, payment institution,
government body or telecommunications operator shall issue, publish or display a
QR code whose payload is an \code{http} or \code{https} URL.
\end{quote}

The public half is categorical. It admits no exception, requires no comparison,
and is evaluated on an observation --- a browser window opened --- that anyone can
make regardless of literacy. The institutional half is a conditional compliance
obligation, which institutions can follow because they have compliance
functions and the public does not.

\paragraph{Why the rule must not be scoped.}
The obvious refinement --- restrict the prohibition to payments and official
communications, leaving restaurant menus and network logins alone --- fails, and
its failure is instructive. To apply a scoped rule, a person must classify the
code before acting on it; but a QR code is not human-readable, so its class is
knowable only after scanning, which is after the browser has opened. A scoped
rule is therefore unusable at the moment the decision is made. The categorical
rule is evaluated after the scan, on what is now visible, and requires no
classification at all. In a population with low general technology literacy this
is not a matter of preference: a rule with an exception is a rule that cannot be
applied.

The hardest test of this rule is not a restaurant menu. It is
\code{verify.gov.kh} (Section~\ref{sec:verifygov}): a government platform, on
official documents, whose entire mechanism is that scanning a code opens a
website. Verification happens in a browser against the platform's servers, so
what the code carries is a reference to be resolved rather than a credential to
be checked \citep{verifygovkh}.

We should be plain about the consequence, because an earlier draft of this
section was not. The two halves do not fall the same way, and only one of them
survives. Scanning a verification code asks the reader to \emph{look at}
something; it asks for no payment, no credential and no personal details, so it
does not violate the \emph{public} rule. The \emph{institutional} prohibition it
violates outright: a government body is issuing QR codes whose payload is an
\code{https} URL, on official documents, at national scale. Our own rule prohibits
the platform in its current form, and we would rather say so than quietly narrow
the rule until it doesn't.

That is not a drafting accident, and the remedy is not an exception. A rule with
a carve-out for official verification codes is a rule the public cannot apply,
for the reason given above: nobody can tell, before scanning, whether the code
in front of them is the official kind. And the public half is falsified in
practice long before it is falsified in logic --- a citizen who scans a diploma
and gets a website has been taught, correctly, that government QR codes open
websites, which is precisely the lesson an attacker needs them to have learned.
The remedy is the composition of Section~\ref{sec:verifygov}: put the signed
credential in the code, and reach the online record, if at all, by some route
that is not the scanned payload.

The same phrasing leaves legitimate URL codes outside the regulated perimeter
uncontradicted. A
restaurant menu never asks for a one-time code, so encountering one does not
teach the public that the rule is false. This matters, because a public rule
falsified by daily experience decays exactly as the browser certificate warning
did.

\paragraph{Why there is no alternative that reaches the same attack.}
Every alternative we are aware of fails for one of two reasons: it requires a
judgement to be made \emph{before or during} the scan, which is precisely the
judgement the population cannot make; or it requires control over software the
state does not have.

The categorical rule is the only intervention in

that nothing better exists; it is a property of where each control sits relative

\paragraph{Who is obliged, and why.}
The public trust that makes this attack profitable is not a natural fact. The
identification of ``QR'' with ``payment'', and of ``payment'' with
``sanctioned by the central bank'', is the output of a successful national
campaign --- it is what adoption at the scale reported in Section~\ref{sec:background}
means. A trust expectation manufactured by policy can only be bounded by policy,
and by the same authority that created it. This is a statement about standing,
not about blame: no other party can make the rule true, because no other party
can bind the institutions whose conduct would otherwise falsify it.

Accordingly the requirement is stated flatly rather than conditionally. Whether
it is adopted is a political question and not a property of the design.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item The prohibition MUST bind the full regulated perimeter --- payment
    institutions, government bodies and telecommunications operators alike.
    A single ministry running a URL-QR campaign falsifies the public rule for
    everyone, and the public cannot be asked to hold an exception.
  \item Legacy printed material MUST be withdrawn within a stated sunset period,
    with an audit. Branch posters, ATM decals and existing marketing are the
    counter-examples most likely to be encountered.
  \item A named regulator MUST hold the obligation, with a public channel for
    reporting violations by institutions.
  \item The public wording MUST be identical across institutions. Variation is
    how a categorical rule erodes into a conditional one.
\end{itemize}

Section~3.2 of the specification is the enforcement half of the same rule inside
the software: a Profile~B payload must not be an \code{http} or \code{https}
URL, and the reference implementation exposes that check as a function intended
to be applied to every scanned code rather than only to KH-SQR ones
\citep{khsqr2026repo}. Neither half is sufficient alone. The software half is
bypassed by the scan path; the policy half has no technical enforcement against
an attacker, only against the legitimate issuers whose compliance makes it true.

\paragraph{What the rule does not do.}
It closes a channel; it does not reduce the adversary's budget. Effort displaces
to what remains permitted --- most obviously to the genuine-code-with-false-pretext
attack of Section~\ref{sec:notachieved}, which is already the dominant vector and
which this rule leaves entirely intact --- and to channels with no QR code at all.
We claim the elimination of one attack, not a reduction in fraud, and the
distinction is the same one this paper insists on everywhere else.

\subsection{A second categorical rule: a payment code is displayed, not printed}
\label{sec:display}

This section is a corollary of the move above, not a second national programme.
It is shorter on institutional detail than Section~\ref{sec:categorical}
deliberately: we specify no threshold, no device subsidy and no compliance
obligation, because the distributional analysis those would need is beyond what
this paper can support. Read it as an argument that the move is available and
that its cost falls somewhere specific, not as a drafted instrument.

The rule in Section~\ref{sec:categorical} empties the set of legitimate
URL-bearing payment codes. The same move is available against overlay forgery,
and it is stronger there than anything in this specification: require that a
merchant-presented payment code be \emph{rendered on a screen at the moment of
payment} rather than printed and left in place.

This does not detect the sticker attack. It removes it. A code that exists only
on a merchant's display for the duration of a transaction cannot be covered by
one that was printed in advance, and --- more to the point --- a printed payment
code becomes recognisable as illegitimate \emph{without authenticating
anything}, exactly as a URL-bearing one does under the first rule. Both are
instances of the first row of Table~\ref{tab:degrees}: act on the meaning before
it is created, by emptying the set of legitimate artefacts, so that recognition
replaces verification.

\paragraph{The rule is unenforceable without the signature, which is the point.}
``Displayed, not printed'' is not a property of a payload and not something a
verifier can observe: a photograph of a screen is a print, and an attacker's
sticker can claim whatever it likes. What makes the rule checkable is that the
point-of-initiation indicator sits \emph{inside the signed prefix}, and that a
dynamic code in this specification carries an expiry no more than 300 seconds
after issuance (Section~\ref{sec:profileA}). A verifier can therefore refuse a
static code outright, and a dynamic one whose window has closed, and neither
refusal can be defeated by printing something that asserts otherwise.

So the operational rule and the cryptographic layer are not alternatives, and
neither is redundant. Without the signature the rule is a request. Without the
rule the signature detects a forgery that the medium would not have permitted.
This is the clearest case in the paper for the two working together, and it is
worth stating because a reader may reasonably ask why a scheme should be signed
at all once codes are dynamic.

\paragraph{The cost is severe and lands where the previous ones did.} A printed
sticker costs almost nothing, needs no device, no electricity and no
connectivity, and that is precisely why KHQR reached the merchants it reached. A
display requirement excludes the market seller, the tuk-tuk driver and the stall
without power --- the same constituency that bears the cost of the exit controls
in Section~\ref{sec:exit}, and a large share of the merchant base rather than
its margin. A rule imposed without subsidised devices would not reduce fraud so
much as reallocate who is allowed to take digital payment at all. We think the
right form is a phased obligation on merchants above a turnover threshold,
paired with provision for those below it, but we are naming a design question
rather than answering it: the distributional analysis it needs is beyond what
this paper can support.

\paragraph{Another national scheme reached the same attack and answered it
differently.} Japan's guideline addresses static-code replacement directly, in
the security part added in its 2022 revision. It observes that because a static
code is printed and installed by the merchant in advance, ``if the installed
static QR code is fraudulently replaced or forged, normal payment will not
occur''. Its remedies are three: print the code on panels or special stock that
is difficult to duplicate; instruct merchants to check periodically whether
another static code has been stuck over the installed one; and instruct
merchants to make a payment themselves from time to time to confirm the code is
still theirs \citep{jpqrmpm}.\footnote{Translations from the Japanese are ours.}

The guideline also states, in the same passage, why it stops where it does:
``because the same static QR code is anticipated to be used multiple times,
anti-forgery measures such as one-time tokens cannot be performed''
\citep{jpqrmpm}. That sentence is exact, and it is the gap this paper's Profile~A
occupies. Freshness cannot protect a reusable artefact --- a one-time token is
consumed, and a printed code is not. A signature is the anti-forgery measure
that does not require freshness: it is verifiable on every scan of the same
sticker for as long as the key is trusted, precisely because it attests
provenance rather than novelty. A national scheme identified the property that
rules out the obvious defence, and did not go on to the one that survives it.

Two things are worth taking from the three remedies. None is cryptographic --- a
scheme with a national mandate reached the same attack this paper's Profile~A
addresses and answered it with stock, inspection and spot-checks. And all three
are directed at the \emph{merchant} rather than the customer, which is a better
choice than it may look: the merchant is the party who loses the payment, who
sees the same code every day, and who can therefore notice a change that no
customer could. Where vigilance is the instrument, pointing it at the party with
the standing incentive is the strongest available version of it.

There is a fourth measure, and it is structural rather than behavioural. The
council operates an application-acceptance system and a printing-and-delivery
service which produces the code mount and stickers to a common design and ships
them to the merchant, and it tells participating providers they need to use both
\citep{jpqrsite}. We put it that way deliberately: the statement appears in the
scheme's onboarding guidance in the register of what a provider needs to do, not
as a requirement in a normative document, and we have not established whether it
binds contractually. What is not in doubt is that a central channel exists and
is the expected route.

We should be careful about what that does and does not make binding, because the
guideline is careful. Printing on hard-to-duplicate panels or special stock appears in a list of
measures that ``there are'' --- examples offered to operators, in a
plainly different register from the obligations elsewhere in the same
document, and confirmed as such by a reader of Japanese. There is no mandated
tamper-evident substrate. What \emph{is}
mandatory is uniformity. The code's design is fixed by the council; minimum cell
size is 0.33\,mm with 0.5\,mm recommended, and printing must be at four dots per
cell or more; and operators ``must not perform any processing, modification,
alteration'' of the design, must tell merchants they may not either, and on
finding a merchant who has, must ``immediately cause said act to cease and
instruct them to use the original'' \citep{jpqrmpm}.

That combination is the instrument, and it is not the one we first took it for.
The binding half is the uniformity, not the channel: whatever the status of the
print service, the design, the cell size and the prohibition on alteration are
stated as obligations, and together they mean the genuine artefact has a single
known appearance.
It does not make a counterfeit hard to manufacture. It makes one \emph{easier to
notice}, because there is a fixed reference to notice a deviation from --- and it
gives the merchant, who is the party the guideline's inspection advice is aimed
at, something definite to compare against. It acts on the provenance and the
appearance of the physical artefact rather than on the payload. Cambodia has no
equivalent: a merchant there may print a KHQR code on anything, at any size, in
any layout, which leaves an inspecting merchant nothing to compare against.

\paragraph{But an absence is only legible once it is unusual.} That is the
limit on uniformity, and it applies equally to this paper's own instrument.
Uniformity and signatures are both \emph{provenance} devices, and what each
finally asks a person to act on is an absence: a code that does not match the
fixed design, a payload that carries no signature. Below saturation, the
absence carries no information. An unsigned KHQR code during a rollout is far
more likely to mean a merchant not yet enrolled than a forgery, and a merchant
or a verifier that learns this learns to proceed. This is the indicator-decay
mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:foreign} arriving from the domestic direction
rather than the cross-border one, and it is worse here, because during a
rollout the benign reading is not merely the common one but the correct one. A
partial deployment of Profile~A is therefore not a proportionate fraction of
its value; for as long as coverage is partial it is close to none, and the
design should be judged on whether it can reach saturation rather than on what
it does at the margin. Section~\ref{sec:limits} records this as an unaddressed
deployment precondition.

So the options against overlay forgery are three rather than two, and they can
be ordered by what they demand. Centralised printing changes the artefact's
provenance and asks a merchant only to obtain codes through one channel; it is
compatible with a printed static code and therefore with the stall that has no
device. The display rule removes the printed artefact altogether and demands a
device. A signature (Section~\ref{sec:profileA}) leaves the artefact alone and
demands a conforming verifier. A deployment may reasonably choose the first
where it cannot afford the second, and the first is the option we had missed.

It is also, precisely, the burden the display rule removes. A code rendered for
one transaction cannot be covered by one printed in advance, so there is nothing
to inspect periodically and no spot-check to remember.

\paragraph{What it does not reach.} Not authorised push payment fraud, which
uses a genuine code the criminal is entitled to display, on a screen as readily
as on paper. Not quishing, whose codes are not merchant payment codes at all.
Not registration abuse. The rule closes one attack completely and leaves the
category this paper identifies as dominant exactly where it was.

\paragraph{And it does not transfer to documents.} A screen cannot be attached
to a diploma or a land title. A printed credential has to carry its own proof,
possibly for decades, which is why Section~\ref{sec:verifygov} argues about
offline signatures rather than about the medium. The two problems look alike
because both involve a printed square, and they are not alike: one instrument is
consumed in seconds and the other must survive its holder.

\subsection{Time-to-list as the operative metric}
\label{sec:ttl}

A mule account's useful life is short: it receives, it forwards, it is
abandoned. The quantity that determines how much passes through it is the
interval between the first institution recognising it and every other
institution being able to act --- \emph{time-to-list}. Detection accuracy matters
less than dissemination latency, because an accurate signal delivered after the
funds have moved has no effect on the loss.

This has an immediate architectural consequence, and it is the reason our risk
list is not built on an eventually consistent store. An eventually consistent
read leaves a window in which a just-listed account still reads clear, and that
window is exactly when the account is being drained. The implementation
therefore routes reads and writes for each account through a serialising object,
with a durable database as the authority.

\paragraph{Rotation is real, and it is bounded.}
The interval is set by the adversary, and one Cambodian case lets it be
estimated rather than assumed. An Anti-Corruption Unit prosecution announced in
March 2026 concerned a deputy chief of customs at a checkpoint on National Road
48 who collected payments from transporters over roughly two years, and who is
reported to have rotated QR codes and receiving accounts deliberately to avoid
detection, using forty-one accounts across six institutions
\citep{cambodianess2026kohkong}. Set against a reported figure of about three
thousand collections, that is on the order of seventy per account: rotation, but
nothing like rotation per payment.

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions. Rotation bounds what
any register can achieve, because a list populated more slowly than the
adversary rotates never binds at all. But rotation does not defeat one, because
each new account costs an identity and the reuse interval is therefore finite
and, on this evidence, not small. The race is between dissemination latency and
rotation interval, and both are measurable quantities rather than matters of
opinion.

The second observation is the sharper one. Forty-one accounts spread across six
institutions is the case against per-institution detection stated as an
observation rather than as a design preference: each institution saw a fraction
of a pattern that was legible only in aggregate, and no single one of them was
in a position to see the whole of it. A register shared between institutions is
not a convenience. It is the only vantage point from which the pattern exists.

\paragraph{What that case is not.} It is extortion collected over the payment
rail, not fraud committed with a QR code: the transporters knew exactly who they
were paying, and that was the problem. We use it for two structural facts --- the
rotation interval and the cross-institution spread --- and for nothing else. It
supports no estimate of QR fraud prevalence, which remains unavailable
(Section~\ref{sec:data}).

\subsection{Screening: the checkpoint the list exists for}
\label{sec:screening}

A register that nobody consults before releasing money is a record of the fraud,
not a control on it. The operative step is therefore a check made by the payer's
institution, at the moment of payment, against the payee account named in the
code.

This is also the one point at which layer~1 feeds layer~3. A verified Profile~A
attestation yields the payee account identifier from a payload that has been
proved unaltered, so the account screened is the account that will be paid. We
should be precise about how much the signature contributes here: the account
identifier would be present in an unsigned payload too, and the risk list would
still work on it. What the signature adds is that the identifier cannot have
been substituted in transit, and that the code's issuer is attributable
afterwards. The control that actually bites on this fraud is the list, not the
signature.

The reference implementation exposes screening as a single call returning a
decision rather than a status, because a status invites each institution to
invent its own mapping, and a national scheme in which one bank holds where
another releases is not a national scheme. The mapping is normative:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{\threecol}
\toprule
\textbf{Status} & \textbf{Action} & \textbf{Character} \\
\midrule
\code{clear}      & Execute & --- \\
\code{restricted} & Hold: delay, or route to manual review & Prudential, reversible, expires in 72 hours \\
\code{blocked}    & Refuse & Standing assertion, two officers \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A low-value carve-out is available --- below a threshold, a payment to a
restricted account may proceed with a prominent warning rather than being held
--- because holding a one-dollar transfer costs a real person more inconvenience
than it denies an attacker. It is disabled by default in every currency. The
safe value is the default, and a regulator who wants less friction must choose
the threshold and own the choice.

\paragraph{A hold is borrowed reversibility.}
The deeper justification for holding rather than merely warning is not caution.
A system whose payments are final has, by construction, no way to undo a
transfer once the payer has been deceived --- and that property, not the quality
of anyone's cryptography, is the variable Section~\ref{sec:japan} identifies as
separating Cambodia's exposure from Japan's. A hold reintroduces a small,
targeted quantity of the missing reversibility: for the narrow set of payments
that trip a signal, and for a bounded period, the transfer is not yet final. It
does not restore general reversibility, which Cambodia gave up deliberately and
for good reasons. It buys back just enough of it, in just the cases where the
loss would otherwise be irreversible.

\paragraph{What is recorded, and what deliberately is not.}
Only decisions with a consequence --- warn, hold, block --- are written down. A
cleared payment leaves no trace, and the payer is never identified to the
service at all. Recording every screening would build a national record of who
paid whom, which is not this system's business and is not needed for its
purpose: whether an account was listed at a given moment is already
reconstructable from the append-only change feed. We note this because a
transaction-screening service is exactly the kind of infrastructure that
acquires surveillance functions by accident, one reasonable-sounding field at a
time.

\paragraph{What happens when the register is unreachable.} Everything else in
this design fails closed: an unverifiable code is refused, a stale trust list is
refused, a missing timestamp statement is treated as hostile. Screening cannot
inherit that rule, and the difference is worth stating rather than leaving to an
implementer.

A verifier that refuses to pay when the risk-list service is unreachable has
converted an availability failure at one national service into a national outage
of retail payment, and has handed any attacker who can degrade that service the
ability to stop the country paying. So screening in the payment path must fail
\emph{open}: on timeout the payment proceeds, the decision is recorded as
\code{unscreened} rather than \code{clear}, and the distinction is preserved in
the record so that an institution can see afterwards which payments were never
checked and reconcile them against listings made in the interval.

This is a real weakening and we do not disguise it. An adversary who can cause
timeouts can pass payments through unscreened, which makes availability of the
register a security property and not merely an operational one --- an argument
for the serialising-object design in this section, and for the delta endpoint
that lets an institution hold a recent local copy rather than depending on a
live call for every payment. The residue is bounded by how long an institution
tolerates operating unscreened, which is a policy parameter we do not set here.

\paragraph{What screening does not do.}
It stops payments to accounts that are \emph{already listed}. The victims of a
mule account during the interval before anyone lists it receive nothing, which
is why time-to-list and not detection accuracy is the metric. It sees the first
hop only, and nothing of cash-out or onward layering. And it sits in the payment
path, so if it is not fast, institutions will find ways around it.

\subsection{Exit controls: attacking the conversion, not the payment}
\label{sec:exit}

Section~\ref{sec:japan} identifies two substrate properties that govern
exposure, and observes that Cambodia holds the worse value of both. Finality is
not available to change: it is why Bakong exists, and Section~\ref{sec:background}
records what it bought. That leaves the liquidity of the output as the one
substrate property still open, and it is the more consequential of the two.

The mechanism is different in kind from everything else in this section.
Screening (Section~\ref{sec:screening}) acts on the \emph{entry}: it decides
whether a payment should be made, and it can only do so for accounts somebody
has already listed. Exit controls act on the \emph{departure}: they do not ask
whether the inbound payment was legitimate, and they require no detection, no
listing and nobody to have been believed. They make the proceeds slow or
impossible to convert.

Three forms, in increasing order of friction:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Withdrawability tiered by verification.} An account at a low
    identity-assurance tier may receive without limit but may not cash out above
    a threshold without stepping up. Japan's prepaid boundary is the strong
    version of this, and its refinement is worth copying: the restriction
    attaches to the value at the moment it arrived, not to the holder's current
    status, so a recipient cannot liberate past receipts by upgrading afterwards
    \citep{paypaybalance}.
  \item \textbf{A cooling window on onward transfer} of recently received funds,
    for accounts in a defined risk class.
  \item \textbf{Velocity limits} on newly opened accounts, and on accounts
    receiving from many unrelated payers for the first time --- the signature of
    a mule account, and one visible without knowing anything about any
    particular payment.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Coerced labour is a reason to prefer this over friction.}
The operations documented in Section~\ref{sec:device} are staffed, on the
researchers' account, by trafficked people compelled to work under threat of
violence. Setting aside what that fact demands of policy on its own terms, it
has a narrow analytical consequence for control selection: the adversary's
marginal labour cost is near zero and is not borne by the adversary. Every
control whose mechanism is \emph{making fraud more effortful} --- additional
verification steps, manual review, friction in the attack path --- is discounted
accordingly. Controls that remove the convertibility of the proceeds are
indifferent to how much labour is available to the attacker, because they do not
operate on the attacker's effort at all.

\paragraph{Why this may matter more than screening.}
Screening protects nobody until an account has been listed, so the victims
between the first fraudulent receipt and the first listing are unprotected by
construction --- and those are the victims that make a fresh mule account
worthwhile. Exit controls bind from the first receipt. The two are complements
rather than alternatives: exit controls cover the window screening cannot reach,
and screening covers the accounts that pass a risk class's tests.

\paragraph{The cost, which is serious and falls on the wrong people.}
Exit friction is friction on cash flow, and small traders live on cash flow. A
market seller who needs today's takings today is precisely who Bakong was built
to serve, and a blanket delay would tax them to inconvenience a fraudster. Any
workable design must therefore bind a \emph{risk class} --- new account, no
history, anomalous inbound pattern --- and leave an established merchant
untouched. We state this plainly because the failure mode is predictable: a
control that is felt by ordinary users and merely irritating to criminals will
be routed around, relaxed, and eventually removed, and it will have cost
something in the meantime.

We have not implemented exit controls. They belong to account infrastructure
rather than to the QR layer, and nothing in this repository touches them.

\subsection{A hold is not a sanction}

The design distinguishes two statuses, and the distinction is a matter of
principle rather than granularity:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Restricted} --- a prudential hold. One institution's operational
    judgement about its own exposure, taking effect immediately, expiring by
    default after seventy-two hours unless renewed.
  \item \textbf{Blocked} --- a standing assertion about an account, requiring
    approval by a second officer before it takes effect.
\end{itemize}

Conflating them would let an operational reflex acquire the force of a penalty.
A hold that never expires is a sanction imposed without process; the expiry is
what keeps it a hold. Removal likewise requires two officers, in both
directions, so that a single compromised or coerced credential can neither
freeze money nor release it.

\subsection{Expiry is evaluated when the status is read}

Statuses carry a stored deadline compared against the clock when someone asks,
and there is no sweep job. This is a deliberate choice about failure direction.
A cron that fails to run would silently extend a restriction on a real person's
account, and neither they nor the institution would observe anything wrong; the
error is invisible and prejudicial. A deadline that has simply passed cannot
fail in that direction.

\subsection{The right to contest a listing}
\label{sec:appeal}

A national register that can freeze a real person's money needs a way for that
person to be wrong about. Expiry, two-officer approval and an immutable audit
trail all reduce the chance of error. None of them lets the affected person do
anything about one.

The design has three constraints, and the third is the substantive one.

First, the customer cannot query this service and cannot appeal to it directly.
An open lookup would tell a mule operator whether their account had been
detected yet, which would convert a fraud control into reconnaissance. The
account-holding institution raises the contest on its customer's behalf.

Second, raising a contest does not change the status. An institution must not be
able to clear a suspicion --- its own or another's --- by asserting that it is
disputed.

Third, \textbf{an unanswered contest lapses the listing.} Raising it starts a
clock against the institution that made the listing: 24 hours for a restriction,
72 hours for a block, in both cases shorter than the listing's own lifetime so
that appealing accelerates something real. If that institution does not answer
before the deadline, the listing ceases to be in force --- evaluated at read time,
like the expiry, with no sweep job. Silence favours the account holder, because
the account holder is the party who cannot act. An institution that wishes to
keep an account listed must be willing to say so, on the record, with a named
officer against it.

\paragraph{An asymmetry we had to correct.}
Building this exposed a defect in the design as originally specified. A
restriction takes one officer to impose but two to remove, which makes an error
more expensive to correct than to make --- the wrong way round for a control that
acts on people who have done nothing. The rule is now narrower: a withdrawal by
the institution that made the listing, in answer to a contest, takes one officer,
because it retracts that institution's own assertion and creates no new one. The
two-officer requirement stays where it belongs, on a discretionary removal by an
institution that did not make the listing. Upholding likewise takes one officer,
so that answering a contest is never more onerous than ignoring it.

\paragraph{An unresolved tension.}
The listing institution's identity is disclosed to the account-holding
institution, not to the customer. This preserves the ability to contest while
respecting the tipping-off constraints that anti-money-laundering regimes
impose, but it means the affected person learns that they are listed and by
which category of reason, without learning who listed them or on what evidence.
We do not think this is satisfactory, and we do not have a better answer that
does not compromise investigations. We record it as an open problem rather than
resolving it by assertion.

\subsection{Attribution, rate limiting and the audit record}

Every write records the institution \emph{and} the individual officer, via
per-officer mutual TLS credentials. An audit entry naming only an institution is
not an audit entry: accountability requires that someone specific be nameable
afterwards. The log is append-only, enforced by database triggers rather than by
convention, and hash-chained so it can be exported as a file whose interior
integrity is checkable without trusting the database it came from, and published
to a transparency log later without redesign. Corrections are new rows.

Writes are rate limited per institution and per officer, and sustained volume
above a threshold is refused \emph{and} recorded as an incident rather than
merely throttled. An institution listing thousands of accounts in an hour is
compromised or misconfigured; neither condition is a reason to act on its
assertions.

\subsection{The institutional layer is a power, and powers are held by people}
\label{sec:integrity}

Everything proposed in this section moves discretion towards institutions and
the officials inside them: a listing that restricts an account, a hold that
delays a payment, an exit control that decides when value becomes cash. We have
argued so far about whether those controls reach the harm. There is a second
question, which is what happens when the control is held badly, and a design
that does not answer it is assuming an honest operator in the same way the
specification was assuming an uncompromised verifier
(Section~\ref{sec:device}).

The same prosecution answers it. The official is reported to have held two
national identity cards, to have registered collection accounts in the names of
close relatives and an associate, and to have paid sums of between one hundred
and twenty-five hundred dollars to have specific reports removed or not
broadcast \citep{cambodianess2026kohkong}. That last detail is the one that
matters here: the capability being described is not fraud but the suppression of
its record, exercised by a person holding an official position.

Three consequences. All three are already in the implementation, and we would
defend none of them as sufficient.

\paragraph{The audit record exists for this, not for tidiness.} A hash-chained,
append-only log naming the individual officer behind every write, exportable as
a file whose interior integrity is checkable without trusting the database that
produced it, is not defensive engineering against a hypothetical insider. Its
purpose is that a listing made for a reason other than the stated one leaves a
record the person who made it cannot afterwards revise. Corrections are new rows
for the same reason. An append-only log that the operator can rewrite is a log
of what the operator wishes had happened.

\paragraph{The right to contest is a control on the lister, not a courtesy to
the listed.} Section~\ref{sec:appeal} presents the appeal path as due process,
which it is. It is also the only mechanism in the design by which a listing made
in bad faith is surfaced by somebody with an interest in surfacing it. This is
why the appeal deadline is evaluated at read time and lapses in the subject's
favour: an institution that simply declines to answer must not thereby win,
because declining to answer is precisely what a bad-faith lister would do.

\paragraph{And it is a reason for the hold to be short.} A restriction that
expires in seventy-two hours unless a second officer renews it is a weaker
instrument against fraud than an indefinite one. It is also a far weaker
instrument against a merchant who declined to pay somebody. We accept the first
cost in order to bound the second, and we record the reasoning here because
otherwise the short expiry reads as timidity rather than as a choice.

\paragraph{What we are not claiming.} Not that this design resists a determined
insider --- an officer who can list can still list wrongly, and the audit record
establishes only that they did. Not that the case above is representative of
anything; it is one prosecution, and the fact that it was prosecuted and
announced is itself evidence of a functioning integrity mechanism. The claim is
narrower and, we think, unavoidable: an institutional control layer proposed for
this environment must be designed as though some of the people operating it will
misuse it, because a case in which exactly that happened is a matter of public
record.

\subsection{Liability allocation}
\label{sec:liability}

The fourth layer is not a security control but an allocation of loss, and its
design determines who has the incentive to reduce the residue.

The United Kingdom's approach is the clearest available precedent. Since 7
October 2024, in-scope authorised push payment scam victims must be reimbursed
up to a maximum of GBP 85,000, with the cost shared equally between the sending
and the receiving payment service provider \citep{psr2024app}. The equal split is
the substantive design choice: it gives the receiving institution --- the one that
opened the mule account and is best placed to detect it --- a direct financial
interest in the quality of its own onboarding, which a sender-pays rule would
not.

We note the precedent rather than recommending its transplantation. Cambodia's
market structure, dollarisation, and the balance between banks and payment
institutions all differ, and a reimbursement cap calibrated to UK incomes has no
meaning elsewhere. What transfers is the principle that liability should rest
where the cheapest available control is, and that a scheme which leaves the
entire loss with the payer has by construction allocated it to the party with
the least ability to prevent it.

\paragraph{One scheme wrote these rules and did not publish them.} Japan's
guidelines on code-payment fraud are detailed and public, and they contain no
allocation of loss at all: nothing on who bears an unauthorised transaction as
between cardholder, merchant, acquirer, operator and issuer
\citep{jpqrfraud,jpqrcard}. That is not an oversight. The association's own
announcement records that responsibility-sharing and compensation for
unauthorised transactions were placed in a separate document, distributed to
council members and not made public.

We draw a narrow conclusion. The technical guidance for a national scheme can be
published in full while the question of who pays is settled privately among the
firms that would pay --- and a merchant deciding whether to accept, or a
regulator assessing where risk sits, cannot read the part that determines the
answer. The UK approach cited above is not better because its cap is
right; it is better because it is a published rule that a party can plan
against. Where liability is unpublished, Section~\ref{sec:data}'s problem
recurs in a different form: the thing that would let anyone evaluate the
arrangement is the thing not disclosed.

We have not implemented anything at this layer. It is policy, and it is named
here because a threat decomposition that stopped at layer~3 would misrepresent
where the residual risk finally settles.

\section{Generalisation}
\label{sec:general}

\subsection{The layer result}

Stated generally: \emph{a control enforced at layer $n$ cannot govern semantics
created at layer $n+1$.} A signature binds bytes to an issuer. If the harm is
produced by the meaning a person attaches to those bytes, and that meaning is
created above the signed layer, then no strengthening of the signature reduces
the harm. Effort spent there is not merely insufficient; it is misdirected, and
it carries the additional cost of producing an assurance that invites the
inference it does not support.

The diagnostic question for any proposed control is therefore not ``is this
mechanism sound?'' but ``at which layer is the harmful meaning created, and is
that at or below the layer where this mechanism is enforced?''

\subsection{Four degrees of freedom}
\label{sec:degrees}

If a control cannot reach the layer at which the harm is created, four moves
remain, and we can identify only four. Each acts at a different point in the
causal chain: before the meaning is created, on the transfer once it is made, on
the value once it arrives, or on who bears the loss at the end.

\begin{widetable}
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.17\linewidth}p{0.13\linewidth}p{0.31\linewidth}p{0.29\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Move} & \textbf{Acts on} & \textbf{Mechanism} & \textbf{Instance} \\
\midrule
Raise the control above the harm &
  The meaning, before it is created &
  Empty the set of legitimate artefacts, so anything outside it is recognisable
  without authentication &
  The categorical prohibition on URL-bearing codes
  (Section~\ref{sec:categorical}); the display-not-print rule for payment codes
  (Section~\ref{sec:display}) \\[0.4em]
Make the consequence reversible &
  The transfer, after it is made &
  Interpose a ledger somebody controls, so a transfer can be undone after the
  deception has already succeeded &
  Japan's operator-held ledger (Section~\ref{sec:japan}); narrowly, the
  prudential hold (Section~\ref{sec:screening}) \\[0.4em]
Remove convertibility of the proceeds &
  The value, after it arrives &
  Let the transfer stand, but ensure what arrives cannot become spendable money &
  Japan's prepaid boundary (Section~\ref{sec:japan}); exit controls
  (Section~\ref{sec:exit}) \\[0.4em]
Reallocate the loss &
  Who pays, after everything &
  Move the cost to the party holding the cheapest available control &
  Liability allocation (Section~\ref{sec:liability}) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{What remains available when a control cannot reach the layer where the
harm is created. The four moves act at successive points of the causal chain:
meaning, transfer, value, loss.}
\label{tab:degrees}
\end{widetable}

The middle two moves are the ones worth dwelling on, because neither contradicts
the layer result --- both sidestep it. Neither governs meaning at all. One governs
\emph{consequence}: the deception still succeeds, the victim is still deceived,
and the transfer is undone from \emph{below} the layer at which the lie was
told. The other governs \emph{convertibility}: the transfer is never undone, and
the fraud still fails, because what the fraudster now holds cannot be turned
into money.

Both are available precisely because consequence and convertibility, unlike
meaning, are properties of the substrate, and a substrate is something a
designer or a legislator chooses.

\paragraph{When the rail does not supply reversibility, somebody sells it.}
The second move is not hypothetical in Cambodia, and it is instructive that it
has appeared as a commercial product rather than as a property of the system. A
Cambodian firm founded after its own founder was defrauded now intermediates
online purchases from abroad, holding the buyer's side of the transaction and
offering a guarantee against non-delivery \citep{wingbank2025espass}. Stripped
of its retail framing, that is an escrow: a party interposed between payer and
payee precisely so that a payment can be undone after a deception has succeeded.
It is the reversibility the push-payment rail removed, reintroduced privately
and sold by the transaction.

Two things follow. The demand is revealed rather than assumed --- people are
paying for reversibility, which is evidence that irrevocability is priced as a
cost by the users who bear it. And the distribution is regressive in a way a
substrate-level property would not be: reversibility becomes an add-on available
to those who can pay for it and who transact through an intermediary that offers
it, rather than a feature of the rail available to everyone on it. The market
seller taking a domestic KHQR payment
(Section~\ref{sec:exit}) gets none of it. A degree of freedom exercised
privately reaches whoever buys it; exercised in the substrate, it reaches
everyone, which is the difference between the Japanese prepaid boundary and an
escrow service.

The distinction between them matters operationally. Reversibility is a
\emph{recovery} mechanism: it works only if somebody notices in time, so it
inherits every weakness of detection. Removing convertibility attacks the
economics directly: if the proceeds cannot become cash, the scheme does not pay
whether or not anyone detects it, whether or not the victim is believed, and
whether or not any account was ever listed.

\subsection{Japan: the same code, a different substrate}
\label{sec:japan}

The QR code was invented in Japan, at Denso Wave in 1994, by a team led by
Masahiro Hara, to track automotive parts; its designers did not exercise their
patent rights, which is the main reason the format became universal
\citep{densowave}. Japan also has substantial code-payment volume
\citep{meti2026cashless}. It does not have Cambodia's QR fraud shape, and the
reason is not better cryptography.

Japanese code payments are commonly operator-mediated stored value: the scan
spends a balance funded in advance, or authorises a linked card, and settlement
to the merchant occurs afterwards across the operator's own books. A major
operator documents exactly this arrangement, including the boundary that makes
part of the balance non-withdrawable \citep{paypaybalance}, and the statute
behind that boundary is public \citep{jpsa}.

We do not claim this is how \emph{most} Japanese code payments work. We have not
audited the market operator by operator, and the argument does not need us to:
what Section~\ref{sec:degrees} requires from Japan is an \emph{existence proof}
--- that a national-scale code-payment scheme can run on a substrate whose
output is not withdrawable money --- and one operator's own documentation
establishes that. A prevalence claim would carry no additional weight for the
argument and would be the one part of this section we could not support.

\paragraph{The symbology is not the substrate, and EMVCo is silent on the
difference.} It is worth being precise about which layer the contrast lives at,
because the two systems are not distinguished by their codes. EMVCo's
merchant-presented specification standardises a payload: identifiers, lengths, a
CRC, an amount field and a currency field \citep{emvco2017mpm}. It says nothing
whatever about what happens to the money afterwards --- whether a ledger is
interposed, who holds it, whether an entry can be reversed, or whether what
arrives can be withdrawn. Two national schemes can therefore share a symbology,
share a specification, and share very nearly a byte layout, while differing
completely in the exposure a payer carries when they scan.

That has a consequence for interlinked schemes which follows from the
architecture rather than from any particular arrangement. Where two schemes
interoperate, the same printed symbol may be resolved by different wallets onto
different rails; and since the substrate governs reversibility and
convertibility, the exposure attaching to that one symbol is a property of
\emph{which rail resolved it}, not of the code. A payer scanning onto an
operator-held prepaid ledger and a payer scanning onto an immediate interbank
push are not running the same risk, and nothing in the symbol distinguishes
their situations --- nor could anything, since the difference is not encoded
there.

This is not hypothetical, and Cambodia is the case. JPQR is Japan's unified
QR-payment standard, established in 2019. JPQR Global, promoted by Japan's
Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry and developed with the Payments Japan
Association, coordinates JPQR with other countries' unified standards so that a
visitor can pay in Japan with their own national service. It launched at the
Expo 2025 site on 5 July 2025, and \emph{KHQR was the first foreign standard it
supported} --- Cambodian users paying at the Cambodia Pavilion, official stores
and other outlets, with Indonesia named as the next addition
\citep{expo2025jpqr}.

So the two substrates this section contrasts are now joined, and the joint is
instructive in three ways.

\emph{First, the convergence is at the instrument layer only.} What
interoperates is the code and the routing. Nothing in the arrangement alters
what either rail does with the money: a Japanese user paying through a domestic
operator remains on that operator's ledger, and a Cambodian user paying through
Bakong remains on an immediate interbank push. The same merchant acceptance
point can therefore serve two payers carrying materially different exposure, and
the difference is invisible in the symbol because it is not encoded there.

\emph{Second, the linkage is intermediated, and the intermediaries are not
visible to the payer.} The published operator list names a scheme body for
planning and operation, an acquirer, and a separate switching-system operator
\citep{expo2025jpqr}. Whatever assurances exist run between those parties. A
payer's wallet cannot check them from the code, and is not shown them.

\emph{Third, the direction matters and is asymmetric today.} What launched is
outbound: a Cambodian paying in Japan. Enabling Japanese services to be used
with overseas unified systems is described as an aim rather than a shipped
service \citep{expo2025jpqr}. We take no position on when that reverses; we note
only that the exposure question is direction-dependent, and that the direction
which would place a Japanese payer on Cambodia's irrevocable rail is the one not
yet in production.

The general form is the point. Format convergence between national schemes does
not produce risk convergence, and a policymaker who reads shared EMVCo
conformance --- or a shared acceptance mark --- as shared exposure has confused
the instrument with the substrate. That is this paper's thesis arriving from the
direction of interoperability rather than of cryptography, and it is the
strongest reason to state the layer boundary in terms a payments official can
act on.

Cambodia's arrangement is the opposite. The scan \emph{is} the settlement
instruction, and the transfer is interbank, immediate and final.

Four consequences follow from that single difference:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item an operator is interposed, holding a ledger it controls and can adjust;
    in an interbank push there is no middle, and nobody positioned to reverse;
  \item the loss is bounded by the funded balance rather than by the account
    balance;
  \item recourse exists --- an operator dispute for prepaid value, and inherited
    chargeback rights where a card funds the wallet;
  \item the set of payees is curated by the operator, so paying an arbitrary
    stranger's account is not the default primitive.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The second property, which matters more.}
Reversibility is not the whole of it, and probably not the important half. The
two substrates also differ in what \emph{comes out}. In Cambodia the payee
receives money: a balance in a bank account, withdrawable as cash, forwardable,
convertible. In Japan the payee typically receives a claim redeemable through a
chokepoint the operator owns.

These two properties are independent, and separating them is the analytically
useful step:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{\threecol}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Finality of the transfer} & \textbf{Liquidity of the output} \\
\midrule
Cambodia (Bakong) & Final; no party can reverse & Money: withdrawable, forwardable \\
Japan (code payments) & Reversible; operator holds the ledger & A claim redeemable at a chokepoint \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent A system may be final and illiquid, or reversible and liquid. Cambodia
holds the worst available combination and Japan the best, but nothing forces
them to move together.

\paragraph{And the illiquidity is statutory, not a product choice.}
This is the part that makes the observation a policy instrument rather than an
architectural accident. Japan's Payment Services Act distinguishes prepaid
payment instruments from funds transfer services; refund of a prepaid balance is
generally prohibited, and a scheme designed to permit general refunds is liable
to be reclassified as a funds transfer service, with the heavier licensing that
entails \citep{jpsa}. Operators therefore have a standing regulatory reason to
keep value non-withdrawable.

The effect is visible in product design. PayPay distinguishes a verified,
withdrawable balance from an unverified, non-withdrawable one --- and value loaded
into the non-withdrawable class stays non-withdrawable even after the holder
subsequently completes identity verification \citep{paypaybalance}. The
restriction attaches to the value at the moment it was loaded, not to the
holder's current status, so a recipient cannot convert previously received funds
by upgrading their account afterwards.

We note, because it bears on how much credit the design deserves, that none of
this was built as an anti-fraud control. It is a licensing boundary drawn for
prudential and consumer-protection reasons, and the closure of the fraud exit is
a side effect. That makes it more interesting rather than less: a property this
valuable arose from a rule written for another purpose, which is a reason to
look at licensing boundaries generally when asking where fraud economics can be
attacked.

\noindent Two qualifications, and both matter.

\textbf{Japan is not free of the deception this paper is about.} Impersonation
and investment fraud are serious there. The money travels by bank transfer, cash
handover or crypto instead. Stored value did not stop deception; it stopped
deception arriving \emph{by QR}. Attackers go where finality is, which is an
uncomfortable way to read Cambodia's exposure rather than a reassuring one.

\textbf{Finality was chosen deliberately, and it bought something.} Removing
chargeback risk is part of why acceptance costs are low enough for a market
stall (Section~\ref{sec:background}). Recommending Japan's architecture would
undo that and insert intermediary rent in its place. We do not recommend it. We
use it to identify the variable, and then --- in Section~\ref{sec:screening} --- to
buy a little of that variable back where it matters most.

We characterise the architecture at the level of its class rather than auditing
individual operators, and we report no Japanese incident figures: the sources we
consulted disagreed on both category and magnitude, and we did not verify them
against the National Police Agency's own releases.

\subsection{The parallel with custody and exposure}

Our earlier work on sovereign hybrid blockchains for regulated DeFi reached the
same shape by a different route \citep{sengtha2026csb}. Base-layer identity
controls secured \emph{custody} of an asset --- who may hold and move it --- and did
not secure \emph{exposure} to it, because the derivative claim conveying that
exposure was minted one layer above the enforcement point. The identity control
was correct and did what it said; the harm simply happened somewhere it did not
reach.

The correspondence is exact:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{\threecol}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Regulated DeFi} \citep{sengtha2026csb} & \textbf{QR payments (this work)} \\
\midrule
Control        & Base-layer identity   & Signature over the code \\
What it secures & Custody of the asset  & Authenticity of the instrument \\
What it misses  & Exposure to the asset & The transaction, and its pretext \\
Why             & Claim minted one layer above & Meaning created one layer above \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two independent domains producing the same failure shape is weak evidence for
generality --- two cases is two cases --- but it is enough to motivate the
diagnostic question above as a design practice.

\subsection{Other push-payment markets}

Where Japan is a contrast, India's UPI and Brazil's PIX are instances. Both are
real-time push-payment systems in which payments are irrevocable and initiated
from a machine-readable artefact --- the same substrate as Bakong --- and both have
contended with the same migration of attacker effort from instrument to
persuasion. Contrast and instance do different work: the instances show the
pattern recurs, and the contrast identifies which property drives it. We do not claim
detailed knowledge of either market's incident data and make no quantitative
comparison. The structural point stands independently of the numbers: where
payments are final and initiation is delegated to an artefact the user cannot
read, securing the artefact is necessary and is not close to sufficient.

\section{Data availability and the absence of Cambodian incident statistics}
\label{sec:data}

We are unable to report the prevalence of QR-code fraud in Cambodia, and we
consider this worth stating explicitly.

There is no published national incident dataset. The figures currently in
circulation --- a widely syndicated claim of a 146 per cent quarter-on-quarter
increase in ``quishing'' in the first quarter of 2026, and similar --- originate in
vendor marketing material. They describe telemetry from the vendors' own
customer bases, with undisclosed denominators, no stated methodology, and no
Cambodian breakdown. They are not evidence for any claim in this paper and we do
not rely on them; we mention them only because a reader will encounter them and
should know why they are absent here.

The vendor material is the visible half of the problem. The less visible half is
that figures with no stated provenance circulate through entirely respectable
channels and become the basis on which people act. A commercial bank's own
publication reports, in passing and without a source, that approximately forty
million dollars was lost to online scams in Cambodia in the first half of 2024
\citep{wingbank2025espass}. We have no reason to think the figure wrong. We have
no way to evaluate it either: no denominator, no definition of what counts as an
online scam, no methodology, no collecting body named. That is not a criticism
of the publication, which was profiling an entrepreneur rather than reporting
statistics. It is what happens in the space an official series would occupy, and
it is why the recommendation below is about publication rather than about
collection.

\paragraph{And the absence is not Cambodia's alone.} We checked whether a
better-resourced scheme does better. Across Japan's merchant-presented and
consumer-presented guidelines, both fraud-prevention guidelines, the summary
deck and the operator bylaws --- and across the scheme's own site and the
association's publication index --- there is no incident count, no fraud rate and
no loss figure for code payments \citep{jpqrmpm,jpqrfraud,jpqrcard}. The
documents describe attack mechanisms qualitatively and quantify none of them.

This matters for how the finding should be read. It is not that Cambodia has
fallen behind a standard others meet. A scheme with a national mandate, a
standards body, published technical guidelines and a decade of operation
publishes no more incident data than one without. The gap is structural to how
payment schemes are governed, which makes it harder to fix by comparison and
easier to fix by rule --- nobody has to be caught up with, and a publication duty
would put its holder in front rather than behind.

\paragraph{The absence is narrower, and more fixable, than ``no data''.} It
would be wrong to say nothing is collected or published. The NBC reports that it
received 721 customer complaints through its hotline in 2025, of which 683 were
resolved, 12 remained in progress and 26 were before the courts
\citep{nbc2025annual}. That is a complaints channel, a count, and a resolution
status, published in the central bank's own annual report.

What it does not carry is a breakdown by category. Nobody reading it can say how
many of the 721 concerned fraud rather than fees, service or account access ---
let alone how many concerned a QR code. The same is true of the Kapea Scams
intake discussed below. So the gap is not collection and not even publication in
the abstract; it is that the published aggregate is undisaggregated, and an
undisaggregated total cannot prioritise anything. Adding a category dimension to
a count that is already gathered and already published is a smaller ask than any
new system, and it is the ask that would change what this section could report.

We should also be plain that 721 hotline complaints is not a national fraud
figure and we do not treat it as one. It is what one channel at one institution
recorded. Its value here is as evidence that the reporting machinery exists and
that the missing element is a field, not an apparatus.

The absence itself is a finding. A payment system at the scale the NBC reports
\citep{nbc2025annual} without public incident statistics cannot support
evidence-based prioritisation: no party can demonstrate that forgery is rarer
than deception, which is the very claim this paper argues on structural grounds
and would prefer to argue on data. Establishing mandatory incident reporting for
payment institutions, with published aggregates, would do more for the design of
countermeasures than any cryptographic improvement to the codes --- including
this one.

That recommendation became substantially cheaper in August 2026, and it is worth
being precise about what remains to be done. A national collection mechanism now
exists: the Kapea Scams application gathers public reports of technology-related
fraud, and its operators state an intention to analyse them for emerging
patterns \citep{khmertimes2026kapea}. What is absent is not the collection but
the \emph{publication}, and the \emph{institutional} half of the collection. The
reported uses are all internal to enforcement. Two additions would convert an
enforcement intake into a national evidence base:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item a duty to publish aggregates on a fixed schedule, by category, so that
    prioritisation --- by anyone, including the authorities themselves --- can be
    argued from evidence; and
  \item mandatory reporting by banks and payment institutions alongside victim
    self-reporting, because institutions observe the money movement that victims
    do not, and because victim reporting under-reports for reasons
    (Section~\ref{sec:trajectory}) that no application design can remove.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Neither is a new institution. Both attach to a mechanism built weeks
before this paper was written, which is when such obligations are cheapest to
impose and hardest to argue against.

Everything empirical in this paper is therefore either a citation to a primary
specification or an artefact that a reader can execute
\citep{khsqr2026repo}. The symbol measurements in Table~\ref{tab:qr} are
produced by a script; the conformance claims are produced by a test suite; the
architectural properties are asserted by continuous-integration checks that we
verified fail on deliberately introduced violations.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{No deployment, no field evaluation.} Nothing here has been
    deployed with real merchants or real users. We report no usability data, no
    scan-reliability data at the larger symbol sizes, and no adoption data.
    Section~\ref{sec:measurement}'s claim that a larger symbol scans less well on
    an inexpensive handset is a reasonable inference, not a measurement.

  \item \textbf{Coverage is a precondition, and we have not shown it is
    reachable.} A signature is a provenance instrument, and what it asks a
    verifier to act on is the \emph{absence} of one. Below saturation that
    absence is uninformative, because an unsigned code is far more likely to be
    a merchant not yet enrolled than a forgery; the value of a partial
    deployment is therefore not a fraction of the whole but close to nothing
    until coverage is near-complete (Sections~\ref{sec:foreign}
    and~\ref{sec:display}). We have no enrolment model, no cost estimate for
    issuing keyed codes across the merchant base, and no account of what a
    verifier should display during the interval in which coverage is partial.
    That interval is the period in which the scheme is most exposed, and we do
    not address it.

  \item \textbf{Mirror independence is unmet.} Section~\ref{sec:mirrors}. The
    implementation does not satisfy a requirement its own specification states.

  \item \textbf{The first container encoding was not legacy-transparent.}
    Section~\ref{sec:container}. Two deviations from EMVCo, one of which
    invalidated the design's stated premise for the choice of template range.
    Encoding version~2 removes both, at a cost of 64 characters and one QR
    version. Version~1 remains in the suite because its vectors are published,
    so a conforming implementation must still read it.

  \item \textbf{The device-compromise evidence is about a different fraud.}
    Section~\ref{sec:device} documents account takeover, not authorised push
    payment fraud, and its victim population is largely outside Cambodia. We use
    it for three narrow claims --- QR codes are an operational delivery vector,
    coached installation defeats passive defences, and device-side verification
    can be subverted --- and for nothing about Cambodian fraud prevalence.

  \item \textbf{We make no claim about the effect of the awareness campaigns.}
    Section~\ref{sec:response} observes what they ask of the public and for how
    long they have run. It does not assert that they failed, because the
    counterfactual is unavailable for the same reason everything else here is:
    there is no outcome measurement. A reader who takes that section as evidence
    of ineffectiveness has read more into it than it says.

  \item \textbf{Exit controls are proposed, not designed or evaluated.}
    Section~\ref{sec:exit} argues that the liquidity of the output is the
    remaining substrate lever, and sketches three forms. We have not specified
    thresholds, defined the risk classes, or estimated the cost to legitimate
    small-trader cash flow, which is real and which we believe would dominate
    the design.

  \item \textbf{The Japanese comparison is architectural, not empirical.}
    Section~\ref{sec:japan} characterises a class of payment architecture and
    reports no Japanese incident data. We did not audit individual operators,
    and we did not verify fraud figures against the National Police Agency's own
    releases. The contrast identifies a variable; it does not measure its effect.

  \item \textbf{The categorical rule is proposed, not adopted or evaluated.}
    Section~\ref{sec:categorical} argues from the structure of the decision
    point, not from evidence that the rule changes behaviour. We know of no
    trial of a categorical QR rule in any jurisdiction, and we have not run one.
    Its cost --- the legitimate URL-QR uses it forecloses inside the regulated
    perimeter --- is real and unquantified here.

  \item \textbf{Screening and appeals are implemented but unevaluated.} The
    endpoints, the status-to-action mapping and the lapse-on-silence rule are
    built and tested (Sections~\ref{sec:screening} and~\ref{sec:appeal}), but we
    have no evidence about false-positive rates, about how often institutions
    would answer a contest in time, or about the latency screening would add in
    a live payment path.

  \item \textbf{Not implemented.} The offline Root ceremony is specified but not
    performed. Liability allocation (layer~4) is discussed and not implemented,
    being policy rather than software. Device attestation for verifier
    authenticity is named as a mitigation and not built. Cross-border trust-list
    federation is out of scope entirely.

  \item \textbf{The template identifiers are only partly verified against other
    schemes.} Encoding version~2 occupies unreserved templates \code{85},
    \code{86} and \code{87}. EMVCo reserves \code{80}--\code{99} but allocates
    nothing inside it, so two schemes may independently choose the same
    identifier for different content and neither would be non-conformant; no
    mechanism in this specification would detect that, because each parser would
    read the other's template as its own and both would be behaving correctly.
    We have checked one of the two schemes that matter. Japan's unified
    merchant-presented guideline allocates \code{80} --- in its local-tax
    profile, as a fixed 85-character extension area --- and mentions no
    identifier between \code{81} and \code{99} anywhere
    \citep{jpqrmpm}. Our three are therefore clear of JPQR. They remain
    unchecked against Cambodia's own guideline, which is the check that matters
    most and which we could not obtain.

  \item \textbf{Truncated key identifiers.} Eight bytes is a size choice, not a
    collision-resistance claim. The specification requires verifiers to treat
    the identifier as a hint and to try every candidate, which contains the
    consequence, but an implementer who ignores that rule has a real bug.

  \item \textbf{One implementation.} The conformance suite exists to enable
    independent ports and has not yet been run against one. Until it has, the
    suite tests our reading of our own specification.

  \item \textbf{No formal verification, no external audit.} The parsers are
    hand-written and differentially fuzzed, which is materially weaker than
    either.

  \item \textbf{The SiteKey evidence is analogical.}
    Section~\ref{sec:indicators}. A 2007 study of website images in a
    non-Cambodian population supports a narrow claim about unnoticed absent
    indicators; it does not establish how Cambodian users respond to QR
    verification interfaces, and we know of no study that does.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We set out to build a signature scheme for Cambodia's national QR payments and
to say precisely what it achieves. It achieves the elimination of forgery: a
code that verifies was produced by a registered issuer and has not been altered,
and the reference implementation demonstrates this against a published
conformance suite whose negative cases outnumber its positive ones.

It achieves nothing whatever against the fraud that currently dominates. A
genuine code, correctly signed and payable to a correctly registered account,
presented with a false story, verifies --- and must. The deception is created one
layer above the enforcement point, and no control at the lower layer reaches it.
That is not a deficiency of this design but a property of layered systems, and
the same property we found in a different domain when base-layer identity
secured custody of an asset and left exposure to it untouched.

The practical implication is uncomfortable for anyone hoping cryptography will
settle the matter. The next marginal improvement in Cambodian QR fraud outcomes
will not come from a better signature. It will come from a categorical rule that
empties the legitimate set of URL-bearing payment codes, which costs nothing to
enforce against the small regulated population that would otherwise falsify it;
from shortening the interval between an institution recognising a mule account
and every other institution being able to act on it; from allocating liability
so that the party best placed to prevent a loss bears part of it; and, before
any of them, from publishing incident data so that the prioritisation can be
argued from evidence rather than, as here, from structure.

Each of those is an intervention at a layer the code cannot reach, which is the
point. The response to a control that stops too low is not a stronger control at
the same height.

One further thing should be said, because it is the finding we would least like
to be right about. The gap between what the authorities control and what the
public trusts is not fixed; it widens, for reasons structural enough to name and
none of which reverse. Part of that widening is this design's own doing: a code
that visibly verifies teaches its user that codes are checked on their behalf,
which is exactly the belief the attack we cannot address depends on. We expect
deployment to reduce forgery and to increase exposure to authorised push payment
fraud, we do not know the sign of the sum, and in the absence of incident
statistics nobody will find out.

A final point about the register in which all of this is meant. Nothing in this
paper is an allegation of error. KHQR was built correctly for the threat model
it was built against, and that model changed underneath it. The public-awareness
advice is accurate advice. The national verification platform is competently
engineered and internationally recognised, and the architectural property we
spend a section on is a choice with a real benefit attached. Our own design is
right about forgery and useless against deception, and we knew that before we
started. Every control examined here does what it says.

That is the condition being described, and it is why it is difficult to act on.
A defect creates a prompt: something is broken, someone is accountable, a review
follows. A correct control that no longer reaches the harm creates no prompt at
all. Correctness is precisely what removes the occasion to re-examine, and
success is what moves the harm out of reach --- so the safest-looking moment is
the one in which the premise is quietly expiring. What this paper asks for is
therefore not remediation but re-examination: of a premise that was true when it
was adopted, and of the measurement that is the only thing capable of revealing
when it stops being true.

We offer the artefact because the artefact is useful and the forgery layer
genuinely needs closing. We offer the analysis because the artefact will be
misread as doing more than it does, and the most valuable thing a specification
can contain is an accurate statement of its own limits.
